# Fermentation

> Fermentation is the anaerobic metabolism of sugar into simpler compounds — alcohols, acids, CO₂ — releasing small amounts of energy.

## What it actually is

Fermentation is **energy extraction from organic molecules without using oxygen as the final electron acceptor** — anaerobic respiration. Organisms (yeast, bacteria, even muscle cells) make ATP by oxidizing part of a molecule and reducing another part of the *same* molecule, instead of using O₂.

ATP — adenosine triphosphate — is the energy-carrying molecule found in every living cell. It captures chemical energy from food and releases it to fuel cellular work. The "energy currency."

So:

- **Respiration** uses O₂ → complete oxidation → CO₂ + H₂O.
- **Fermentation** doesn't → partial oxidation → organic byproducts (ethanol, acids, etc.).

In sourdough we get **alcoholic** fermentation (yeast) and **lactic** fermentation (LAB) running side by side.

## The two players

### Yeast (e.g. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Candida milleri*)

- Eats simple sugars, produces CO₂ and ethanol
- Responsible for leavening and aroma

### Lactic acid bacteria — LAB (e.g. *Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis*)

- Prefer maltose, produce lactic and acetic acids
- Responsible for sourness, preservation, and crumb complexity

> Both yeast and LAB *ferment* things. But they produce different outputs and work on different sugars. Fermentation isn't just about gas — it's also about acid and flavor.

## The chain in baking

When you say "fermentation" in baking, you mean this chain of transformations:

1. **Enzymes (amylase)** make fermentable sugars.
2. **Yeast & LAB** consume them anaerobically.
3. **Products**:
   - **CO₂** → leavening
   - **Ethanol** → evaporates in oven, contributes to crust aroma
   - **Organic acids** → flavor and gluten conditioning
   - **Esters, aldehydes, ketones** → aromatic complexity
4. **Energy release** drives yeast metabolism (ATP formation).

Fermentation is therefore both a chemical conversion *and* a biological life activity.

## The chemistry inside the yeast cell

The glycolytic pathway (Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas):

```
Glucose ──(10 enzyme steps)──> 2 Pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH
```

Then under anaerobic conditions:

```
2 Pyruvate + 2 NADH ──(yeast enzymes)──> 2 Ethanol + 2 CO₂ + 2 NAD⁺
```

Yeast regenerate NAD⁺ so glycolysis can keep running. That recycling step *is* fermentation.

### Step-by-step

1. **Glycolysis** — Glucose → 2 Pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH
2. **Decarboxylation** — 2 Pyruvate → 2 Acetaldehyde + 2 CO₂ (via pyruvate decarboxylase)
3. **Reduction** — 2 Acetaldehyde + 2 NADH → 2 Ethanol + 2 NAD⁺ (via alcohol dehydrogenase)

Energy yield: **2 ATP per glucose**. (Aerobic respiration extracts ~36 ATP from the same glucose — fermentation only captures ~5% of the available energy. The rest goes into the ethanol and the flavor compounds, which is exactly what gives us beer and bread aroma.)

## Reaction chains side by side

### Alcoholic fermentation (yeast)

```
C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 CH₃CH₂OH + 2 CO₂
(glucose)   (ethanol)
```

**Products:**

- Ethanol → evaporates in bake, contributes aroma
- CO₂ → trapped by gluten → rise and oven spring
- Small esters, aldehydes → fruity, buttery aromas

### Lactic fermentation (LAB)

**Homofermentative** (most *Lactobacillus* species):

```
C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 CH₃CHOHCOOH + 2 ATP
(glucose)   (lactic acid)
```

→ 1 glucose → 2 lactic acid. No gas. Clean, yogurt-like acidity.

**Heterofermentative** (*Leuconostoc*, *Weissella*, some *Lactobacillus*):

```
C₆H₁₂O₆ → CH₃CHOHCOOH + (CH₃COOH or C₂H₅OH) + CO₂ + 1 ATP
```

→ lactic acid + acetic acid (or ethanol) + CO₂. Tangy, aromatic, complex sourness.

### How they interact in dough

| Aspect            | Yeast (alcoholic)        | LAB (lactic / hetero)               |
|-------------------|--------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Main substrate    | Glucose, maltose         | Glucose, maltose                    |
| Products          | CO₂, ethanol, esters     | Lactic + acetic acids, sometimes CO₂ |
| Effect on pH      | Minimal                  | Strongly acidifies                  |
| Effect on flavor  | Fruity, buttery, alcohol esters | Tangy, nutty, mildly sharp   |
| Effect on texture | Gas for leavening        | Acids tighten gluten, improve keeping quality |

They share sugars: **yeast dominates when warm; LAB take the slow path when cool or acidic.** That's why a warm-start → cold-finish method yields balance: first yeast power, then LAB depth.

### Simplified joint equation for sourdough

```
C₆H₁₂O₆ → (C₂H₅OH + CO₂)_yeast + (CH₃CHOHCOOH + CH₃COOH)_LAB
        =  ethanol + CO₂        +  lactic acid    + acetic acid
```

That's the chemical fingerprint of sourdough fermentation.

## Outside of food

Fermentation in chemistry can refer to any microbial transformation of organic matter into a stable compound — ethanol, lactic acid, butyric acid, acetone, methane, etc. That's why we say:

- "Lactic fermentation" — yogurt, sourdough
- "Alcoholic fermentation" — beer, wine, bread
- "Acetic fermentation" — vinegar
- "Butyric fermentation" — rancid butter
- "Malolactic fermentation" — wine maturation

All mean the same thing: **biological redox transformations without oxygen.**

## TL;DR by perspective

| Perspective | Fermentation means…                                                   |
|-------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Chemical    | Anaerobic extraction of energy from sugar; internal redox without O₂. |
| Biological  | Microbes using sugar for ATP and producing organic acids or alcohols. |
| Baking      | Yeast + LAB converting sugar → CO₂, ethanol, acids, aroma.            |
| Cultural    | Controlled microbial transformation that makes food more flavorful, safe, and digestible. |
