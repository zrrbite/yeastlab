# Bread vs beer — same enzymes, same tricks

Bread and beer both rely on the same fundamentals:

- **Amylase** to create fermentable sugars
- **Yeast** to consume them
- **Timing and temperature** to shape sugar retention vs breakdown

Many sourdough techniques borrow their logic — knowingly or not — from brewing science. Enzymes like amylase and protease behave similarly in both, and the idea of controlling sugar availability is a shared tactic.

## Side-by-side process map

| Process stage         | Beer brewing                                                        | Bread baking                                                                |
|-----------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Grain activation**  | Malted barley is mashed at ~65 °C to activate amylase, breaking starch into fermentable sugars | Cold dough rest activates amylase slowly, converting starch into maltose |
| **Fermentation timing** | Brewers may stop fermentation early to preserve residual sugars and body (e.g. for sweeter beers) | Bakers may shorten bulk to preserve sugars for browning, crust flavor, or aesthetics |
| **Yeast role**        | Yeast consumes glucose and maltose, producing alcohol + CO₂        | Yeast consumes sugars, producing gas for rise and flavor-active metabolites  |
| **Sugar management**  | Mash temp & time control attenuation (how much sugar gets fermented) | Cold rests and bulk length control how much sugar remains at bake time      |
| **Flavor compounds**  | Esters (fruity), diacetyl (buttery), fusels (solventy) from yeast stress or nutrient balance | Same: esters, diacetyl, acids — shaped by time, temp, hydration, nutrient stress |
| **Final transformation** | Boiling stops fermentation and fixes sugar level                | Baking kills microbes but the Maillard reaction rapidly consumes remaining sugars for crust color and aroma |

## Brewing chemistry vs sourdough chemistry

### Beer fermentation (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*)

- **Sugar metabolism.** Glucose and maltose are fermented into ethanol and CO₂.
- **Acid production.** Acetic and fatty acids form as byproducts of acetyl-CoA metabolism.
- **Ester formation.** Alcohol acetyltransferases (Atf1 / Atf2) link alcohols (ethanol, fusel alcohols) with acids → fruity esters.
- **Control factors.** Temperature, yeast strain, pitching rate, oxygen, pressure.
- **Goal.** Balance fruitiness with malt and hop character (varies by style).

### Sourdough fermentation (yeast + LAB consortium)

- **Sugar metabolism.** Yeast produce ethanol + CO₂; LAB ferment sugars to lactic and acetic acids.
- **Acid production.** Mostly extracellular by LAB.
- **Ester formation.** Occurs in the dough matrix when yeast-derived alcohols react with LAB acids (enzymatically or spontaneously).
- **Control factors.** Temperature, hydration, flour composition, fermentation duration.
- **Goal.** Depth, roundness, and subtle fruity/buttery complexity in aroma and crust.

## Cross-pollination ideas

A few "what if" ideas where brewing thinking informs baking (and vice versa):

- **"Cold mash."** The DEAM early cold rest is essentially a slow, low-temperature mash — amylase has time to convert starch into maltose without involving the yeast yet. Brewers' single-infusion mash at 65 °C is the same idea, just sped up by heat.
- **Attenuation control.** Brewers manage how dry a beer ferments by mash temp choices (more β-amylase = more fermentable sugar = drier; more α-amylase + dextrins = sweeter). Bakers manage residual crust sugar by cold-rest length and bulk timing.
- **Yeast pitch rate ↔ inoculation %.** Both are knobs on the same dial: less yeast → longer fermentation → more time for flavor compounds to accumulate (and more risk of off-flavors if the culture isn't healthy).
- **Diacetyl rest.** Brewers hold beer warm at the end of fermentation so yeast cleans up diacetyl into less-detectable 2,3-butanediol. Bakers do the opposite — keep the early phase warm to *build* diacetyl, then cool to preserve it before pH falls and degrades it.
- **Water chemistry.** Brewers obsess over ion profiles (Burton-on-Trent vs Pilsen water). Bakers can borrow the same thinking — see [`water-minerals.md`](water-minerals.md).

## Flavor pathway (shared)

```
Enzymes      → Sugars + Amino Acids
Yeast        → Alcohols
LAB          → Organic Acids
Alcohols + Acids → Esters
Heat + Amino Acids + Sugars → Maillard Compounds
All Together → Deep crust aroma, complex crumb flavor
```

The pathway is the same. What differs is **what gets locked in** (boiling vs baking) and **what continues** (yeast and LAB stay alive in beer until packaging; in bread, baking ends the show).
