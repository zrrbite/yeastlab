# Enzymes: the hidden workforce

Enzymes aren't alive, but they make fermentation possible by unlocking food for the microbes and shaping dough texture over time.

## The two that matter

### Amylase

- Breaks **starch** into sugars (especially **maltose**, the LAB favorite)
- Active even at cold temperatures
- Two flavors:
  - **α-amylase** — main player in beer mashing (~55–65 °C / 131–149 °F). Breaks starch into random bits including glucose and dextrins. Helps initial sugar availability and crust browning. Still contributes a bit at room temp.
  - **β-amylase** — more active at room temp, especially during a cold rest. Slowly releases maltose over time → feeds LAB → supports flavor and steady fermentation.

### Protease

- Prefers **acidic environments** — most active towards the end of fermentation (post-bulk cold rest)
- Breaks gluten into peptides and amino acids
- Enhances extensibility and contributes to browning
- Over time, can weaken dough structure if left unchecked

> **Insight.** Enzymes don't need heat or yeast to work. They're active even when your dough is cold — slower, but the Double Enzymatic Activation method uses that to its advantage.

## The amylase paradox

Amylase is essential for breaking starch into maltose, but **too little or too much can ruin your dough in opposite ways.**

**Not enough amylase:**

- Starch stays intact
- Binds water tightly in the matrix
- Water can't escape → gummy crumb
- Less sugar available for yeast or browning
- Crumb may feel dense or underbaked despite a good bake time

→ Result: dough feels wet, but it's actually water trapped in starch.

**Too much amylase:**

- Starch over-degraded into sugars
- Water released, but structure collapses
- No starch gel → crumb can't set
- May brown quickly due to excess sugars, but interior stays sticky or fragile

→ Result: structure disintegrates — gummy, slack crumb with fast crust browning.

> The goal isn't to "activate enzymes" — it's to **tune amylase activity** so starch converts just enough for sweetness and oven spring, but still holds its gel-forming, water-binding role.

## The autolytic process

When flour and water are mixed (no salt, no starter yet):

1. **Enzymes activate immediately**
   - Protease starts softening gluten bonds slightly
   - Amylase begins converting starch to sugars
2. **Gluten starts forming on its own**
   - Water hydrates glutenin and gliadin proteins
   - Over time, they naturally align into gluten strands without physical effort
3. **Dough becomes more extensible and less sticky**
   - Later mixing is easier and more effective
   - You need less kneading to reach good structure

### Cold autolysis

The early cold rest in DEAM is a kind of autolytic phase:

- Hydrates flour gently
- Allows enzymatic activity to begin
- Develops structure passively over hours
- Minimizes stickiness and preserves gluten strength

> "Autolytic" just means structure develops on its own through hydration and time. No kneading needed — just water and patience.

## How gliadin + glutenin become gluten strands

Wheat flour contains two main storage proteins:

1. **Glutenin** — long, elastic chains. Strength and resistance.
2. **Gliadin** — shorter, flexible coils. Extensibility (stretchability).

Water hydrates both, allowing them to:

- Unfold and become mobile
- Form hydrogen bonds, disulfide bonds, and other interactions
- Link together into a 3D mesh network

It's not like aligning bricks in a row — it's more like:

- Hydrated glutenin chains entangle and crosslink
- Gliadin molecules fill the gaps, lubricating and allowing movement
- As you mix, stretch, or fold, the structure becomes more ordered
- Long, continuous strands of gluten form, trap gas, and resist tearing

Mental model:

- **Glutenin** = springs (tight and strong)
- **Gliadin** = slime (fluid and stretchable)
- **Together** = a resilient, elastic net that expands and traps bubbles

## Yeast & sugar metabolism

Yeast loves glucose, but flour doesn't provide much directly. Flour's amylase converts starch into **maltose** — a disaccharide.

To feed, yeast must split maltose into two glucose molecules using its own enzyme, **maltase**:

```
Maltose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) → 2 Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)
Glucose → CO₂ + ethanol + flavor byproducts
```

- Glucose is yeast's primary fuel for gas and alcohol production.
- If maltose production is slow (e.g. short enzymatic rest), yeast may lag.
- A cold rest helps boost maltose availability, setting up stronger fermentation later.

> Give amylase time to work — you're feeding your yeast indirectly by building up maltose first.

## Hydration ↔ enzyme activity

Water is the medium for all enzymatic activity in dough. If there isn't enough water:

- Amylase can't break down starch to sugars efficiently
- Yeast and LAB can't move or metabolize efficiently
- Gas doesn't expand well — tight crumb, slow rise
- **Low hydration = sluggish microbes and weak enzymatic priming**

Conversely:

- High hydration → faster starch and gluten breakdown → faster sugar release → quicker fermentation
- But: if the flour's structure can't hold that water (weak gluten, soft wheat), the dough loses gas before it can lift

## Enzymes & whole grains

- White flours (especially bleached) have **low enzyme activity**.
- Whole wheat and rye naturally contain more amylase and protease, which:
  - Accelerate sugar release
  - Boost fermentation
  - Loosen tight dough

That's why adding just **5–10% whole wheat or rye** can make a big difference in flavor and fermentation speed — especially in a long cold rest. In DEAM, levains get a small dose of whole grain for enzymatic "fuel."
