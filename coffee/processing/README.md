# Coffee processing

What happens between **picking the cherry** and **shipping the green bean** to the roaster. This is the part of coffee that most casual drinkers don't think about — and the part where a coffee gets most of its identity. Two coffees from the same farm, same harvest, processed differently, can taste like wildly different beans.

Processing is **fermentation choice**. The cherry contains pulp, mucilage, and beans — and the microbes acting on the mucilage during processing produce acids, esters, and other flavor compounds that get absorbed into the bean and carried into the cup. The "method" is really about *how much fermentation* and *under what conditions*.

## The methods

- **[`washed.md`](washed.md)** — minimal fermentation. Cherry pulped immediately, mucilage washed off, beans dried clean. Cleaner, brighter, more terroir-driven cup.
- **[`natural.md`](natural.md)** — full fermentation. Whole cherry dried intact, fermenting on the bean for weeks. Heavy fruit, wine, sometimes funky character.
- **[`honey.md`](honey.md)** — middle ground. Pulp removed but mucilage left on the bean during drying. Variable based on how much mucilage stays (white, yellow, red, black honey).
- **[`anaerobic.md`](anaerobic.md)** — modern technique. Cherry or pulped beans fermented in sealed vessels (no oxygen), often at controlled temperatures. Wild, distinctive flavor expression.
- **[`experimental.md`](experimental.md)** — carbonic maceration (whole cherry under CO₂), thermal shock, lactic / yeast inoculation, double fermentation. The frontier.

## What's actually happening biologically

In all processing methods, three microbe groups dominate the fermentation:

- **Lactic acid bacteria (LAB)** — same family as in sourdough and beer. Produce lactic acid, contribute soft-acid sweetness, sometimes diacetyl (buttery).
- **Yeast (wild)** — *Saccharomyces*, *Pichia*, *Hanseniaspora*, *Candida*. Produce alcohols and esters from sugars in the mucilage.
- **Acetic acid bacteria** — convert ethanol to acetic acid. Some present in all methods; dominant when oxygen is plentiful (natural processing in dry climates).

The **method** controls:

- **How much mucilage / sugar is available** (washed = none, natural = all, honey = some)
- **How long fermentation runs** (washed = hours, natural = weeks)
- **How much oxygen is present** (washed = high, natural = high, anaerobic = zero)
- **Temperature** (ambient in most, controlled in modern anaerobic)

→ which determines which microbes dominate → which determines flavor profile.

This is the same chemistry framework as [bread fermentation](../../theory/fermentation.md) and [beer dry hopping](../../beer/hops/dry-hopping.md). Coffee processing is just **fermentation engineering applied to coffee cherries.**

## Process → flavor map

A rough mapping of method to typical sensory outcome:

| Method                       | Acidity      | Body         | Sweetness    | Funk / fruit | Cleanliness |
|------------------------------|--------------|--------------|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| Washed                       | High         | Light–med    | Medium       | Low          | High        |
| Wet-hulled / "Giling Basah"  | Med          | Heavy        | Low          | Low–med      | Med         |
| Honey (white)                | Med-high     | Medium       | Med-high     | Low–med      | Med-high    |
| Honey (yellow)               | Medium       | Medium       | Med-high     | Med          | Medium      |
| Honey (red / black)          | Med-low      | Med-heavy    | High         | Med-high     | Med-low     |
| Natural / Dry                | Med-low      | Heavy        | High         | High         | Variable    |
| Anaerobic (washed style)     | High         | Med-heavy    | High         | Med-high     | High        |
| Anaerobic natural            | Variable     | Heavy        | Very high    | Very high    | Variable    |
| Carbonic maceration          | High         | Medium       | High         | Wine-like    | High        |

These are **tendencies, not rules.** A skilled producer can make a clean natural or a funky washed; the method is a starting point.

## Why processing matters in 2026

Two big shifts in the last decade made processing matter more than ever:

1. **The "third wave" specialty coffee movement** demanded more interesting cups → producers experimented with longer fermentations, controlled-environment fermentations, novel methods.
2. **Lab analysis got cheap.** Producers can now test their fermentations for pH, °Brix, microbial populations → fermentation became a science instead of a folk craft.

Result: coffees in 2024–2026 are more **distinctively processed** than ever, with growing categories like "anaerobic naturals," "carbonic maceration," and "thermal shock" that didn't really exist as commercial categories 10 years ago.

A modern specialty coffee bag should always tell you the **processing method** — and increasingly, sub-method details (e.g., "72-hour anaerobic, lactic-inoculated"). That's a sign of a producer who's intentional about fermentation.

## How to taste-train for processing

The fastest way to learn what each method tastes like:

1. **Buy a flight from one origin** processed multiple ways. Several specialty roasters offer this (e.g., a single Colombian farm in washed, honey, natural, anaerobic). Apollo's Banner, Onyx, Sey, and others do this regularly.
2. **Brew identically across all four.** Same recipe, same water, same brew method. The processing is the only variable.
3. **Take notes blind.** Have someone else label the cups. Identify each method by taste alone.

After 10–20 of these flights you'll be able to taste a single cup and reliably guess the processing method within a category. It takes practice; it's worth it.
