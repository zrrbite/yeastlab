# Natural (dry) processing

The oldest method. The cherry is dried whole — beans inside the fruit — over **2–6 weeks**. Fermentation runs the entire time, intensely flavoring the bean. Naturals are **fruit-forward, heavy-bodied, sweet, and often funky** in a way washed coffees never are.

## What happens

Whole cherries are spread out and dried, with the bean fermenting inside the intact fruit the whole time. The bean absorbs sugars, acids, and aromatic compounds from the surrounding pulp. Drying is **slow and exposed** — to sun, weather, and ambient microbes.

## Step by step

### 1. Cherry harvest

Same as washed — ripe red cherries, sorted to remove unripe and overripe.

### 2. Float separation (optional)

Some producers float-sort cherries before drying; many skip this step in traditional naturals.

### 3. Drying

Whole cherries spread on:

- **Patios** — concrete, the traditional method. Common in Brazil, Yemen, Ethiopia.
- **Raised beds** — wire-mesh African beds, allows airflow underneath. Better for naturals than patios, especially in humid climates.
- **Tarps / tents** — provide shade or rain cover. Protect against unexpected weather.

Cherries are **turned 4–8 times daily** to prevent uneven drying and mold. Drying takes **2–6 weeks** — much longer than washed (5–25 days) because the whole cherry is much wetter than just-mucilaged parchment.

### 4. Continuous fermentation during drying

This is the defining process. As cherries dry slowly:

- **Wild yeast** on the cherry surface ferment sugars in pulp → ethanol + esters
- **LAB** produce lactic acid → lowers pH inside cherry
- **Acetic acid bacteria** convert ethanol → acetic acid (vinegar)
- The bean **absorbs** these compounds through the parchment and silver skin
- Aromatic compounds **diffuse** from pulp into bean over weeks

By the end of drying, the bean has absorbed a substantial flavor load that will carry into roasting and the cup.

### 5. Hulling

Once cherries reach **10–12% moisture**, the dried fruit (now leathery, dark) is mechanically hulled — separating the dried pulp/skin from the bean. Result: green coffee bean with no parchment intermediate step (unlike washed, which has a parchment phase).

### 6. Sorting and resting

Beans sorted by density, defects removed, then rested in storage 1–3 months for stabilization before export.

## What you taste

Natural-process coffees are associated with:

- **Heavy fruit** — strawberry, blueberry, mango, pineapple, dried fruit, often "jammy"
- **Sweetness** — sucrose, ester-derived
- **Wine character** — fermentation alcohols + acids = wine-like aromatics
- **Heavy body** — fuller, denser mouthfeel than washed
- **Funk** — at the more fermented end: fermented fruit, slight booze, sometimes onion / garlic / rotten fruit notes
- **Less acidity** than washed — what acidity is there is **softer**, often described as "fruit acidity" rather than "citrus"
- **Lower clarity** than washed — flavors blend together

Common descriptor families:

- **Berry** (blueberry, strawberry, blackberry — *the* natural signature)
- **Stone fruit** (over-ripe peach, plum, mango)
- **Dried fruit** (raisin, fig, date)
- **Wine** (red wine, port, sometimes brandy)
- **Cocoa / chocolate** (especially in darker roasts of naturals)
- At the funky end: **kombucha, overripe banana, pickled fruit**

## Where it's from

Natural processing dominates in regions where:

- **Water is scarce** — washing requires significant water; naturals don't
- **Climate is dry** — necessary for the long drying period without mold
- **Tradition predates infrastructure** — wet mills are infrastructure investments

Major natural-producing origins:

- **Ethiopia** — many of the famous Yirgacheffe / Sidamo / Guji naturals. The home of natural processing.
- **Yemen** — possibly the original natural process region. Mocha-style coffees.
- **Brazil** — vast majority of Brazilian coffee is natural; pulped natural is also common.
- **Burundi, Rwanda** — increasingly producing experimental naturals.
- **Costa Rica, Colombia** — producing modern controlled-naturals as the experimental category grows.

## Quality challenges

Natural is **harder to do well** than washed:

- **Mold** — slow drying + ambient humidity = real risk. Lots can develop "moldy" or "musty" off-flavors.
- **Inconsistency** — long fermentation under variable conditions (sun, humidity, microbes) = lot-to-lot variation.
- **Defect carry** — defective cherries can't be removed during fermentation; they ferment along with the rest.
- **Storage stability** — dried-fruit residue in dried cherries can carry off-flavor compounds. Some naturals "fade" faster than washed.

Historically, "natural" was synonymous with "lower quality" because it was associated with less-developed producing regions and less-controlled processes. **Modern specialty natural processing has changed this completely** — top-tier naturals from Ethiopia, Yemen, and modern controlled-natural producers are some of the most expensive coffees in the world.

The difference is **process control**:

- Better cherry selection
- Raised beds vs patios
- Frequent turning
- Active mold monitoring
- Climate-aware drying (covering cherries during rain or peak sun)
- Sometimes shade-drying instead of full sun

## Modern variations

### Pulped natural / honey

Bridge between washed and natural. Cherry is **pulped** (skin removed) but **mucilage left on the bean** during drying. See [`honey.md`](honey.md).

### Raisin process

Cherries are **deliberately over-ripened** on the tree before harvest, then dried as natural. More raisin / port-wine character than typical natural. Some Ethiopian and Yemeni styles use this.

### Anaerobic natural

Cherries fermented in **sealed vessels (no oxygen)** for 24–96 hours **before** drying. Then dried as a natural. Produces the modern "wild fermentation" naturals — dramatic fruit, often with kombucha / fermented vibes. See [`anaerobic.md`](anaerobic.md).

### Carbonic maceration

Borrowed from wine. Whole cherries fermented under **CO₂** for 1–8 days before drying. Different microbial population (CO₂-tolerant) → different flavor outcomes. See [`experimental.md`](experimental.md).

### "Black honey natural"

Hybrid: cherry partially pulped (some mucilage left), then dried as a natural-style. Different color naming than honey processing — confusing terminology, common in Costa Rica.

## What "good natural" looks like

A great natural shows:

- **Bright, distinct fruit notes** (specific — not just "fruity") — strawberry, blueberry, mango, lychee, etc.
- **Sweetness** — should be pronounced
- **Some acidity** — not as sharp as washed, but present
- **Body** — full, not heavy or sluggish
- **Clean fermentation** — no rotten / mushroom / muddy notes
- **Aromatic complexity** that develops as the cup cools

A poor natural shows:

- **Muddy, non-specific fruit** — generic "berry" without distinct character
- **Over-fermented** — vinegar, acetone, "fermented socks"
- **Funky in a bad way** — onion, garlic, mushroom, rot
- **One-note "fermenty"** — overwhelming wine character that dominates everything else
- **Drying / astringent** — acetic acid overload

## Pairing with brewing methods

Naturals shine in:

- **Pour-over** (V60 especially) — clarifies the fruit notes
- **AeroPress** — brings out the sweetness
- **Cold brew** — fruit-forward naturals make exceptional cold brew (Ethiopian especially)
- **French press** — heavy-bodied naturals love full-immersion

Less ideal for:

- **Espresso (modern light-roast)** — fermentation character can be too dominant; the sweetness comes through but acidity may be too soft for standard 1:2 espresso ratios

## A note on cupping naturals

Naturals show **dramatic differences** as they cool:

- Hot: aroma intense, acidity present, body felt
- Warm: fruit notes peak, complexity highest
- Cool: sweetness dominates, lingering finish

If a natural tastes "boring" hot, **wait 5 minutes**. Many naturals are best at 50–60 °C.
