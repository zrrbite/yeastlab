# Experimental coffee processing

The frontier. Producers and researchers in the 2020s are pushing coffee fermentation in directions borrowed from wine, beer, kimchi, and food science generally. Some techniques have stabilized into commercial categories; others are still finding their footing.

This file is a survey of the modern experimental landscape — short overviews of each, with cross-references for the techniques that have their own files.

## Carbonic maceration

Borrowed directly from **Beaujolais wine**. Whole cherries fermented under **CO₂ pressure** for 1–8 days.

### How it differs from anaerobic

- **Pure CO₂ atmosphere** vs the "low-O₂" of basic anaerobic (where airlock release of CO₂ creates a mostly-but-not-perfectly anaerobic environment)
- **Pressure** can be applied in some setups (3–6 bar)
- **Selects for CO₂-tolerant microbes** — different population than typical anaerobic
- **Intracellular fermentation** — CO₂ environment drives **enzymatic fermentation inside the cherry's intact cells** before microbial fermentation takes over. Wine science shows this produces different aromatic compounds.

### Cup character

- Wine-like — red wine, port, Beaujolais nouveau (the inspiration)
- **Excellent clarity** despite heavy fermentation — carbonic typically tastes "cleaner" than open-vessel anaerobic
- High acidity, often grapey
- Tropical fruit common
- Body: medium

### Producers and origins

Costa Rica was the early adopter (around 2014–2016 with Sasa Sestic + Diego Bermudez collaborations). Now widespread in Colombia, Costa Rica, Panama, El Salvador. Pricey — among the most expensive specialty coffees.

## Thermal shock

Coffee is **heated** at one or more stages of processing to:

- Denature some enzymes (stop pectinase, stop microbial enzyme activity)
- Kill some microbes (selective — only certain strains die at given temps)
- "Lock in" a fermentation profile

### Variations

- **Pre-fermentation thermal shock** — cherries are heated (40–60 °C in water) before fermentation begins. Sterilizes; selects for thermo-tolerant microbes only.
- **Post-fermentation thermal shock** — after fermentation, beans are blanched in hot water (60–80 °C) to halt fermentation immediately at a chosen point.
- **Drying-stage heating** — controlled-temp drying (>40 °C) instead of ambient sun drying.

### Cup effect

Variable — depends on when in the process the heat is applied. Generally produces:

- More **consistent** results (less microbial variability)
- Sometimes **muted** complexity (heat kills some flavor-active microbes)
- Sometimes **enhanced** body and sweetness (caramelization-like effects)

A producer-specific technique. Different farms get different results.

## Lactic fermentation

Producer **inoculates** the fermentation with a specific **Lactobacillus** culture (like a sourdough starter, but for coffee).

### How

- Cherry or pulped beans go into vessel
- Producer adds a known LAB starter culture (often from a lab, sometimes from an established prior batch)
- Fermentation runs anaerobic, **dominated by the inoculated LAB**
- Produces **lots of lactic acid** and lactic-acid-derived flavors
- pH drops fast, fermentation halts when LAB stop tolerating acidity

### Cup character

- **Yogurt, kefir, milky** notes — direct from the lactic acid
- Soft, rounded acidity
- High sweetness perception
- Less fruit funk than wild fermentation
- **Cleaner and more reproducible** than wild anaerobic

### Why producers do this

Reproducibility. With a known LAB strain dominating, the producer can target a specific flavor profile reliably batch-to-batch — instead of hoping wild microbes deliver consistent results.

## Yeast inoculation

Same idea, with yeast. Producer adds a specific **Saccharomyces cerevisiae** strain (often a wine yeast like **EC-1118**, **Lalvin K1V**, or a brewing strain).

### Cup character depends heavily on strain

- **Wine yeast** (EC-1118, K1V) — wine character, fruity esters
- **Beer yeast** (Saison, Belgian strains) — fruity / spicy esters
- **Sake yeast** — distinctive; rare in coffee but exists
- **Champagne yeast** — bright, lifted aromatics

### Co-inoculation with LAB

Some producers inoculate **both** a yeast and a LAB simultaneously, mimicking the natural sourdough/beer microbial consortium. Produces complex layered fermentation.

## Co-fermentation with fruit / sugar additions

Producer adds **other ingredients** to the fermentation vessel:

- Fruit pulp (mango, passionfruit, raspberry, lychee — common)
- Sugar (cane sugar, panela)
- Wine or other fermenting liquids
- Spices (rare; cinnamon, vanilla)

### Why

The added input either:

- **Drives specific microbial behavior** — different sugars favor different microbes
- **Directly adds flavor** — fruit aromatics absorb into the bean during fermentation

### Controversy

This is the most contested area of experimental processing. Critics argue:

- **It's flavor adulteration** — the cup tastes of the added fruit, not the coffee
- **It's misrepresentation** — coffee labeled "tropical" or "raspberry" is borrowing those notes from added inputs, not from the bean
- **It corrupts the specialty coffee idea** of expressing terroir and bean variety

Defenders argue:

- **It's no different than wine yeast inoculation** — engineering the fermentation
- **The flavor outcomes are real and complex** — not simple "added fruit"
- **The producer is honest about it** when labeled correctly

The current consensus among most specialty buyers (2025–2026): **co-fermented coffees are legitimate but should be labeled as such**, and shouldn't be allowed in competitions like the World Brewers Cup that are meant to showcase coffee terroir.

## Multi-stage fermentation

Coffee goes through **two or more sequential fermentations**, sometimes with intermediate steps (washing, drying partially, re-soaking).

### Examples

- **Diego Bermudez "double anaerobic"** — anaerobic ferment, wash, second anaerobic ferment, then dry
- **"Wash + anaerobic"** — washed coffee that's then anaerobically fermented after washing
- **Triple-stage** — anaerobic → aerobic → anaerobic, layered for complexity

### Cup character

Layered, intense, complex — the "engineered cocktail" of coffee. Difficult to characterize because each producer's process is different. **Almost always pricey.**

## Whisky / wine barrel-aged

Coffee fermented or finished in **used whisky, wine, rum, or beer barrels** that retain residual flavor compounds.

### How

- Used barrel (cleaned but residual character) is the fermentation vessel
- Or: dried green coffee is **stored** in the used barrel for weeks/months before roasting

### Cup character

- Picks up barrel character — whisky notes, oak, vanilla, smoke depending on the barrel
- Can be subtle to overwhelming
- More common in **finished products** (barrel-aged roasted coffee) than in green processing

## Slow-drying / controlled-environment drying

Drying the bean is itself a fermentation/processing step. Modern producers control:

- **Drying time** — 2 weeks to 2+ months
- **Temperature** — ambient, refrigerated, or actively heated
- **Humidity** — covered, ventilated, or controlled
- **Sun exposure** — full sun, dappled shade, full shade

Slower drying = more fermentation in the bean = more developed flavor compounds.

The **Carmo de Minas microlot revolution in Brazil** (around 2010) was partly built on **shade drying**, which produced sweeter, more complex Brazilian coffees than sun-dried equivalents.

## What's coming

Educated speculation on the experimental frontier in 2026+:

- **Designer microbial cultures** — genetically-selected strains for specific flavor outcomes
- **More wine-industry crossover** — punching down, malolactic fermentation, oak techniques
- **Pressure / temperature combinations** that mimic wine maturation processes
- **Bio-monitoring** — pH, Brix, specific microbial population sensors during fermentation
- **AI-assisted recipe design** — predictive models for fermentation outcomes

This is genuinely an interesting time in coffee processing. The 2030s will likely have categories that don't exist yet.

## How to navigate experimental coffees as a buyer

A few practical heuristics:

- **Read the label carefully.** A good producer labels exactly what they did (e.g., "72-hour anaerobic, lactic-inoculated, single-stage washed").
- **Vague labels = caution.** "Special process" without details = reason to ask the roaster.
- **Try things, but don't pay $30/bag for your first.** Start with $15–$20 experimental bags from trusted roasters.
- **Buy from roasters who taste-buy.** Roasters who actually taste their experimental coffees before buying tend to deliver better experimental products than those who buy on producer reputation alone.
- **Some experimental coffees taste weird, not good.** It's OK to find a coffee technically interesting but not personally enjoyable.
- **Take notes.** Build a personal map of what you like and don't like.
