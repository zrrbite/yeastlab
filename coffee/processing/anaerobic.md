# Anaerobic processing

The defining modern processing method. Coffee fermented in a **sealed vessel with no oxygen** (or very low oxygen), often at controlled temperature, for hours to days. Produces dramatically distinctive cups — wild fruit, kombucha, lactic notes — that traditional washed and natural can't replicate.

Anaerobic processing emerged commercially around 2014–2016 and exploded in the 2020s as producers learned to control it. By 2026, "anaerobic" is a standard category on specialty coffee menus alongside washed, natural, and honey.

## What's different about no oxygen

In aerobic fermentation (washed, natural, honey), the dominant microbes are:

- **Aerobic / facultative yeasts** (use O₂ when available)
- **LAB** (facultative — can ferment with or without O₂)
- **Acetic acid bacteria** (require O₂ to convert ethanol → acetic acid)

In **anaerobic** fermentation, oxygen is excluded:

- **Acetic acid bacteria are suppressed** — much less acetic acid (vinegar) production
- **Strict anaerobic microbes can dominate** — including specific LAB species that produce different acid profiles
- **Yeast metabolism shifts to alcoholic fermentation** — more ethanol, more esters
- **Slower, more controlled fermentation** — temperature can also be controlled

Result: a cup with **less sharpness, more sweetness, more aromatic complexity** than the equivalent aerobic processing — sometimes by a lot.

## The standard anaerobic processes

### Anaerobic washed

- Pulped beans + mucilage placed in sealed vessel
- Fermented **24–96 hours** at controlled temp (typically 18–25 °C)
- After ferment: washed clean (removes mucilage)
- Dried as washed coffee

Cup: **very clean, very sweet, lifted aromatics**. Signature is a **lactic, yogurt-like sweetness**. Body still light-medium like washed.

### Anaerobic natural

- Whole cherries placed in sealed vessel
- Fermented **24–96 hours** at controlled temp
- After ferment: dried as whole cherry (natural-style)
- Long, slow drying continues fermentation

Cup: **wild, fruit-bomb, often funky**. Heavy fermentation character — kombucha, fermented berry, sometimes alcoholic. The most extreme processing currently in commercial production.

### Anaerobic honey

- Pulped beans + mucilage placed in sealed vessel
- Fermented anaerobically for 24–48 hours
- Dried as honey (with mucilage on)

Cup: **between anaerobic washed and anaerobic natural**. Heavier than washed, less wild than natural. Honey sweetness with anaerobic complexity overlay.

### Carbonic maceration

A specific case borrowed from wine. **Whole cherries fermented under CO₂.**

- Cherries placed in vessel
- CO₂ pumped in (purges all O₂)
- Fermented at controlled temp for 1–8 days

Different microbial population than typical anaerobic (specific CO₂-tolerant strains dominate). Produces wine-like, very fruity coffees with **excellent clarity** despite heavy fermentation. See [`experimental.md`](experimental.md).

## How it's done in practice

### Equipment

- **Sealed vessel** — barrels (used wine/whiskey/rum barrels common), stainless tanks, food-grade plastic IBCs
- **Airlock** or **bung** to release CO₂ from fermentation while excluding air
- **Temperature control** — varies from "ambient cellar" to active heating/cooling
- **pH meter, thermometer, Brix meter** — for monitoring

### A typical process

1. **Cherry intake and sort** — same as any process
2. **Optional pulping** (if anaerobic-washed or anaerobic-honey)
3. **Loading the vessel** — beans/cherries packed in
4. **Optional inoculation** — some producers add specific yeast strains, LAB, or even fruit pulp to drive fermentation in a chosen direction
5. **Sealing** — vessel closed with airlock
6. **Fermentation** — 24–96 hours, monitored by pH, Brix, and temperature
7. **End of fermentation** — opened, beans extracted
8. **Wash, dry, hull** — depending on style (washed / natural / honey)

### Monitoring

Modern anaerobic producers track:

- **pH** — typically drops from ~5.5 to ~4.0 over fermentation. Producer ends fermentation at target pH (often 4.0–4.2).
- **Brix** — dissolved sugar in mucilage drops as microbes consume it
- **Temperature** — controlled to favor desired microbial populations
- **Time** — usually 24–72 hours; some specialty producers go to 96+ hr

The ability to **measure** transformed coffee processing from folk craft to engineering. A modern anaerobic producer can repeatably produce a target flavor profile.

## What you taste

Anaerobic-processed coffees are associated with:

- **Lactic / yogurt** notes — characteristic of long LAB-dominant fermentation
- **Wild / fermented fruit** — strawberry jam, kombucha, fruit leather
- **Tropical** — pineapple, passionfruit, mango (especially in anaerobic naturals)
- **Wine / cocktail** — red wine, Bellini, Mai Tai (especially carbonic maceration)
- **Sweetness** — usually pronounced
- **Aromatic complexity** — often "more aromatic" than the same bean processed conventionally

Common descriptor families:

- **Stone fruit / tropical** at the gentle end
- **Strawberry yogurt, kombucha, fermented berry** in the middle
- **Wine / champagne / cocktail** at the wild end
- **Cinnamon / spice / floral** in some carbonic macerations

## Where it's from

Anaerobic processing is geographically widespread because it's an **infrastructure choice** more than a climate choice:

- **Colombia** — major innovator, lots of experimental anaerobics
- **Costa Rica** — early adopters; many top anaerobics from CR farms
- **El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua** — increasing production
- **Ethiopia, Kenya** — cautious adoption; traditional processes still dominate but anaerobic experiments exist
- **Brazil, Indonesia** — adopting at scale

The "anaerobic capital" is arguably **Colombia** in the late 2010s and 2020s — producers like Diego Bermudez (Finca El Paraiso) became famous for pushing the technique to extreme outcomes (200+ hour fermentations, multi-stage processes, fruit additions).

## Risks and quality challenges

Anaerobic is **higher risk than washed**:

- **Off-fermentation** — wrong microbes dominating can produce vinegar, butyric acid (rancid), propionic acid (sweat)
- **Inconsistency** — small parameter changes (temp, time, starting pH) can dramatically shift flavor
- **Stuck fermentation** — pH drops below ~3.5 and microbes die; fermentation halts
- **Contamination** — mold or unwanted strains can take over
- **Oxidation on opening** — sudden O₂ exposure when vessel opens can produce acetic acid spike

Modern anaerobic producers manage these with:

- Sterile vessel preparation
- Temperature control (often refrigerated rooms)
- Yeast / LAB inoculation (introduces known cultures, dominates over wild microbes)
- Real-time pH monitoring
- Quality controls at intake (rejecting damaged or under-ripe cherry)

The best anaerobics are **engineered** rather than improvised.

## Modern variations

### Multi-stage anaerobic

Two or more sealed-vessel fermentations in sequence, sometimes with a wash between, sometimes with different inputs (added fruit pulp, sugar, etc.). Pioneered by Diego Bermudez and Sasa Sestic.

### Inoculated anaerobic

Producer adds specific commercial yeast strain (often from wine industry — Saccharomyces cerevisiae cultivars) or LAB starter. Forces the fermentation in a known direction.

### Thermal shock

Coffee is **heated** before or after anaerobic fermentation. Hot water shock denatures some enzymes, kills some microbes, locks in certain flavor compounds. Used by producers who want to "freeze" a fermentation profile.

### Fruit-co-fermented

Whole cherries fermented anaerobically **with added fruit pulp** (mango, passionfruit, etc.). The added fruit drives specific aromatic outcomes. Controversial — purists argue this is "flavor adulteration"; defenders say it's just engineering the fermentation environment. Either way, the cup picks up the added fruit character.

## Pairing with brewing methods

Anaerobics are usually **showcased on filter brewing**:

- **V60 / pour-over** — clarifies the wild aromatics
- **AeroPress** — brings out sweetness
- **Cold brew** — fruit-forward anaerobics make exceptional cold brew

Less ideal for:

- **Standard espresso** — many anaerobics are too aromatic and too acidic for traditional espresso ratios; modern light-roast espresso can work but takes practice
- **Dark-roasted anaerobics** — tend to lose the processing character to roast

## What "good anaerobic" looks like

A great anaerobic shows:

- **Distinctive character** — should taste **different** from a conventional version of the same bean. If it doesn't, why pay the premium?
- **Defined notes** — specific aromatics, not muddy
- **Clean fermentation** — funky/wild is OK if controlled; sour/rotten is not
- **Sweetness** — should be pronounced
- **Aromatic intensity** in the cup as it cools — should keep developing

A poor anaerobic shows:

- **Muddy fermentation** — funk without character
- **Sourness** — too much lactic / acetic acid
- **Mushroom / rotten / "off" notes** — wrong microbes won
- **Inconsistency cup-to-cup** — production wasn't controlled
