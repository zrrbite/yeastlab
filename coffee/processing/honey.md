# Honey processing (pulped natural)

The middle ground between washed and natural. **Skin removed, mucilage left on the bean during drying.** The amount of mucilage retained — and how long the bean dries with it — gives you the **white / yellow / red / black** honey sub-categories. Each behaves differently in the cup.

## What happens

Cherry is **pulped** (skin and pulp removed mechanically, like washed) but the **mucilage** is **not washed off**. The mucilage-coated bean is then dried, with fermentation continuing in the mucilage layer throughout the drying process.

The name "honey" comes from the **sticky, golden-brown appearance** of the drying beans — they look like dripping honey on the patio.

## Step by step

### 1–2. Cherry harvest, sort, float

Same as washed.

### 3. Pulping

Cherry fed through pulper → skin and pulp removed → bean covered in mucilage retained.

### 4. (Sometimes) partial mucilage removal

Producer chooses how much mucilage to remove with a **demucilager** machine or by light wash:

- **White honey:** ~80–100% of mucilage removed. Closest to washed. Quick drying.
- **Yellow honey:** ~50–75% removed. Some mucilage on bean.
- **Red honey:** ~25–50% removed. More mucilage, more fermentation in drying.
- **Black honey:** 0–25% removed. Almost full mucilage. Closest to natural. Longest drying.

The percentages are rough — naming is **producer-defined** and varies by region. The key is that **more mucilage = more fermentation during drying = more "natural-like" cup**.

### 5. Drying with mucilage

Beans dried on patios or raised beds. **Slower than washed** because the sticky mucilage retains water. **Faster than natural** because there's no whole cherry to dry.

Drying time:

- **White honey:** ~1 week (close to washed timing)
- **Yellow honey:** ~10 days
- **Red honey:** ~2 weeks
- **Black honey:** ~3 weeks (close to natural timing)

During drying, mucilage **continues fermenting** — wild yeast, LAB, acetic bacteria all active. Bean absorbs aromatic compounds from mucilage.

The longer the drying, the more fermentation flavor in the bean.

### 6. Hulling

Once dry, mucilage flakes off; bean hulled to remove parchment.

### 7. Sorting, resting, export

Same as washed / natural.

## What you taste

Honey-processed coffees combine traits of washed and natural in proportions corresponding to mucilage retention:

| Sub-style          | Typical character                                      |
|--------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| **White honey**    | Closer to washed: bright, clean, light body, mild sweetness |
| **Yellow honey**   | Balance of brightness and sweetness, medium body      |
| **Red honey**      | More body, more fruit/honey character, less acidity    |
| **Black honey**    | Closer to natural: heavy fruit, strong sweetness, full body, sometimes funky |

Common descriptor families across honeys:

- **Honey** (literally — golden, sticky sweetness)
- **Caramel / brown sugar** (sweetness + Maillard)
- **Stone fruit** (peach, apricot — moderate)
- **Tropical fruit** (in red/black honeys)
- **Floral** (jasmine, honeysuckle — in white/yellow)
- **Cocoa** (in darker roasts)

## Where it's from

Honey processing is most associated with:

- **Costa Rica** — modern home of honey processing as a category. Costa Rica's microlot revolution in the 2010s built around honey naming conventions.
- **El Salvador** — significant honey production.
- **Brazil** — pulped natural (their term for what's essentially red honey) is widespread.
- **Honduras, Nicaragua, Panama** — increasing honey production for specialty markets.
- **Colombia** — some experimental honeys at the high-end farms.

In **Brazil** specifically, "pulped natural" predates the "honey" naming convention and is essentially the same process. Older texts call it pulped natural; newer ones call it honey. Same thing in practice.

## Why honey vs washed or natural

Producers choose honey processing for several reasons:

- **Differentiation** — a producer with washed and natural lots can offer honey as a third style for buyers
- **Risk management** — naturals are higher-risk (mold, defects); honey is safer with similar flavor benefits
- **Water savings** — versus washed (no mucilage washing required)
- **Specialty market premium** — well-processed honeys command higher prices than washed from the same farm
- **Climate fit** — works in regions where rainfall makes natural drying risky but water is too precious for full washing

## Sub-style decisions

Choosing **between** white, yellow, red, and black honey depends on:

- **Bean varietal** — high-acid varietals (Geisha, SL-28) often shine as white/yellow honey where their acidity is preserved; low-acid heirlooms can be pushed to red/black for more body
- **Producer's flavor goal** — clarity → white; complexity → red; fruit-bomb → black
- **Climate during drying** — humid climate favors lighter honeys (faster drying = less mold risk); dry climate allows darker honeys
- **Processing capacity** — black honey takes longer; producer needs drying space and patience

## Quality considerations

Honey processing is **moderate-difficulty** — easier than natural, harder than washed:

- **Mold risk** during slow drying — especially with darker (more mucilage) honeys
- **Uneven fermentation** if mucilage retention isn't consistent across the lot
- **Sticking together** — beans can clump during drying without frequent turning
- **Roasting unpredictability** — honey-processed beans roast slightly differently than washed (more residual sugars → more Maillard, faster color development)

Modern honey producers manage these with raised beds (better airflow), careful turning schedules, and sometimes covered-but-vented drying setups.

## What "good honey" looks like

A great honey shows:

- **Pronounced sweetness** — should taste like its name
- **Clean fermentation** — no muddy / vinegary notes
- **Defined fruit** (in red/black) or **defined acidity** (in white/yellow)
- **Body that matches the sub-style** — light to full corresponding to mucilage retention
- **Smooth finish** — should not be drying or harsh

A poor honey shows:

- **Muddy / undefined sweetness** — sweetness without character
- **Over-fermented** — vinegar, sour, drying
- **Inconsistent across the cup** — bright at first, muddy at the end
- **Mold / mustiness** — drying problem

## Pairing with brewing methods

Honeys are flexible:

- **White / yellow honey** → pour-over, AeroPress, light filter brewing
- **Red / black honey** → pour-over (still works), French press, espresso
- **Across the spectrum** → cold brew works well; the sweetness dominates
