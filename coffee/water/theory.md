# Coffee water — theory

Why each ion matters, and why two waters with the same TDS can produce wildly different cups. This is the chemistry layer underneath the [recipes](recipes.md). Read this once and the recipes stop being arbitrary numbers and start being levers.

## What "extraction" is, briefly

When water hits ground coffee, it dissolves a fraction of the soluble compounds — caffeine, organic acids, sugars, melanoidins (from roasting), aromatic oils, polysaccharides, minerals. About **18–22%** of the bean's mass extracts into the cup under typical brewing.

**Under-extraction** (< 18%) tastes sour, weak, hollow. **Over-extraction** (> 22%) tastes bitter, drying, harsh. The water you brew with **shifts which compounds extract preferentially** and how fast — which is why the same beans can taste different through different waters.

## The four ions that matter most

### Magnesium (Mg²⁺)

The brightness ion.

- **What it does in coffee:** Mg²⁺ has a higher affinity for **flavor-active organic acids** (citric, malic, lactic, chlorogenic) than calcium does. It "binds" to these compounds during extraction, helping them transfer into the cup.
- **Sensory effect:** brighter, more acidic, more *expressive* coffee. Higher perceived complexity. Especially noticeable in light-to-medium roasts where the bean's intrinsic acidity is the headline.
- **Threshold:** below ~5 ppm Mg you'll notice flatness; 10–25 ppm is a typical specialty-coffee target; above ~40 ppm it can taste metallic or "sharp."
- **Why third-wave recipes lean Mg-heavy:** Maxwell Colonna-Dashwood and Christopher Hendon's *Water for Coffee* (2015) made the case for high Mg : Ca ratios for filter brewing. The "Bright" recipes lean on this.

### Calcium (Ca²⁺)

The body ion.

- **What it does in coffee:** Ca²⁺ extracts in coffee similarly to Mg²⁺ but with **less affinity for organic acids**. Instead it tends to bind to **larger molecules** — proteins, polysaccharides, melanoidins (the brown roasting compounds).
- **Sensory effect:** more body, more "weight," more **mouthfeel**. The coffee feels fuller. Less expressive acidity than Mg-heavy water.
- **Threshold:** 10–30 ppm is typical. Below ~10 ppm = thin cup; above ~50 ppm = heavy, can mute brightness.
- **Caveat: scale.** Ca²⁺ + HCO₃⁻ + heat → calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) precipitation = **scale buildup**. This is the espresso-machine killer. Every espresso recipe with notable Ca²⁺ must keep bicarbonate near zero, or accept that the machine will scale.

### Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)

The acid-buffering ion.

- **What it does in coffee:** bicarbonate **neutralizes acids** in the cup. It reacts with H⁺ ions (the source of perceived sourness) to form CO₂ + H₂O. The bicarbonate disappears in the process.
- **Sensory effect:** softer, "rounder," less acidic perception. Higher coffee pH after brewing.
- **The trade-off:** if you love the bright complexity of a light-roasted Ethiopian, bicarbonate will *blunt* it. If you want a mellower cup that's easier to drink black, some bicarbonate helps.
- **Espresso machine concern:** bicarbonate + Ca²⁺ + heat = scale. **Modern espresso recipes target zero bicarbonate.**
- **The third-wave dogma:** for filter coffee with light-roast specialty beans, **most baristas recommend zero or very low bicarbonate** (< 30 ppm). For everyday "good but easy-drinking" coffee, 40–50 ppm is fine.

### Sodium (Na⁺)

The neutral ion.

- **What it does in coffee:** Na⁺ doesn't really *extract* anything (no chemical affinity for coffee compounds). It primarily **shifts perceived taste** — slight saltiness can amplify perceived sweetness and reduce perceived bitterness. Classic culinary trick.
- **Sensory effect:** rounder, slightly more "complete" cup. Subtle.
- **Threshold:** < 10 ppm = imperceptible; 10–30 ppm = subtly enhances; > 50 ppm = noticeably salty (unwelcome).
- **Practical role:** in DIY recipes, sodium usually arrives via sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) — included for the bicarbonate but bringing Na⁺ along. Most zero-bicarbonate recipes are also low-sodium.

## Less-critical but worth knowing

### Sulfate (SO₄²⁻)

In coffee water, sulfate is mostly the **counterion to magnesium** (when you add Epsom salt, you're adding Mg²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ in equal molar amounts). Sulfate has **minimal direct flavor impact** in coffee at these concentrations — unlike in beer, where sulfate sharpens hop bitterness.

### Chloride (Cl⁻)

Mostly the counterion to calcium (from CaCl₂). Like sulfate, **minimal direct effect** on coffee flavor at typical concentrations.

### Chlorine and chloramines (Cl⁻ as oxidizer)

**Different from chloride!** Chlorine and chloramines are **disinfectants** added to municipal water supplies. They:

- React with organic compounds → produce chlorophenols (medicinal, band-aid off-flavors)
- Damage the rubber seals in espresso machines over time
- Should be **completely removed** before use in coffee

Activated carbon filters (Brita, plumbed-in carbon block) remove chlorine effectively. RO filtration removes both chlorine *and* chloramines. **If your tap water is chlorinated, use filtered or bottled water for coffee — full stop.**

### TDS (Total Dissolved Solids)

A bulk measure of all dissolved minerals. Useful as a **sanity check** — you can buy a $20 TDS pen to see roughly where you are. A typical specialty-coffee water target is **120–180 ppm TDS**.

But TDS alone doesn't tell the whole story. Two waters at 150 TDS can be wildly different:

- 150 TDS of mostly Mg + Ca + a bit of Na (clean specialty profile) → great coffee
- 150 TDS of mostly bicarbonate + chloride (typical municipal water) → mediocre coffee, scale buildup

**TDS is a check, not a target.** The *composition* of the dissolved solids matters more than the total.

## How extraction temperature interacts with water

- **Hot water (90–96 °C / 195–205 °F)** — typical brewing range. Extracts most compounds in 2–6 minutes (espresso to French press).
- **Cold water (4–22 °C)** — extracts much more slowly (12–24 hours for cold brew). Selectively extracts **less of the acids** and **more of the sugars/aromatics** — which is why cold brew tastes smoother and sweeter than the same beans hot.

Water mineral content matters at all temperatures, but the **effect is most dramatic in espresso** (short contact, high pressure, high temperature) and **least dramatic in cold brew** (long contact eventually equilibrates).

## Why one water doesn't fit all roasts

A few practical observations:

- **Light roasts** (Scandinavian roasts, third-wave specialty) — high acidity, complex aromatics, often single-origin. Want **Mg-forward, zero bicarbonate** to express the brightness.
- **Medium roasts** (most specialty filter coffee) — balanced. Most recipes work; SCA standard or Colonna-Dashwood Bright are good defaults.
- **Dark roasts** (traditional Italian, French) — already low-acid, more bitter, more body-driven. **Some bicarbonate** softens any remaining edge, fits the style.
- **Espresso blends** (often medium-dark) — body matters, machine scale matters. **Zero bicarbonate, moderate-to-high Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺.**

## What "good extraction" looks like across the cup

A well-extracted cup has:

- **Bright acidity** without being sour
- **Sweetness** that lingers
- **Body** appropriate to the brew method (heavier in French press, lighter in V60)
- **A clean finish** — not muddy, not bitter, not lingering harsh
- **Aromatic complexity** that you can pick out specific notes from

Bad water (or wrong-water-for-the-bean) typically shows up as:

- **Flat / hollow** — under-mineralized water, often distilled or near-zero TDS
- **Sour** — under-extraction, but can also be Mg too high without enough Ca / body
- **Bitter** — over-extraction, but can also be too much bicarbonate + heat building astringent compounds
- **Drying / metallic** — too much Mg, too high TDS, or chlorine in tap water
- **Salty** — too much sodium
- **Muddy / heavy** — too much Ca, too high TDS, or under-filtered
- **Boring / one-note** — could be many things, but minerals out of balance is high on the list
