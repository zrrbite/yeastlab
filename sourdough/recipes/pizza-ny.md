# NY-style pizza dough (DEAM)

Big, foldable, crisp-on-the-outside, chewy-on-the-inside slices — the kind you can pick up and bend without the tip flopping. Built for a **6–8 minute bake at 260–290 °C**, which is what most home ovens with a stone or steel can actually deliver.

If you have a 450 °C+ pizza oven, see the [Neapolitan recipe](pizza-neapolitan.md) instead. NY style was *invented* for slower, lower-temp ovens — that's its whole identity.

## What "NY style" actually means here

- **Bread flour, not 00.** High-protein (~12.5–14%) for chew and structure across a 6–8 min bake.
- **A little oil. A little sugar.** Both help browning at lower temps when there's less Maillard horsepower than a 500 °C deck.
- **Slightly drier dough.** 60–63% hydration. Easier to hand-toss into a big, even circle.
- **Hand-stretched, but stretched bigger.** 14–16" pies are the target.
- **Long, slow ferment.** 48–72 hr cold rest is the secret. Way longer than Neapolitan because home-oven char doesn't compensate for shallow fermentation.

## At a glance

| Phase                  | When            | Time     | Temp        |
|------------------------|-----------------|----------|-------------|
| Pre-ferment wake-up    | 3 days before   | ~1 h     | Room temp   |
| Final mix              | 3 days before   | 15 min   | Room temp   |
| Bulk + folds           | 3 days before   | 2 h      | 22–24 °C    |
| Ball + cold rest       | 3 days before → bake day | 48–72 h | 4 °C |
| Final proof            | Bake day        | 1.5–3 h  | Room temp   |
| Bake                   | Showtime        | 6–8 min  | 260–290 °C  |

## Formula (baker's percentages)

For **4 × 280 g balls** (14" pies):

| Ingredient            | Bakers % | Grams |
|-----------------------|---------:|------:|
| Bread flour (12.5–14% protein) | 100% | 700 g |
| Water                 | 62%      | 434 g |
| Salt                  | 2.0%    | 14 g  |
| Sugar                 | 2.0%    | 14 g  |
| Olive oil             | 3.0%    | 21 g  |
| Cold-fed levain (100% hydration) | 8% | 56 g |

**Total dough weight:** ~1239 g → 4 × 280 g balls. (Bigger balls = bigger pies; scale to your peel and oven.)

> **Why these numbers.** 62% hydration gives a dough you can throw and shape without it slacking out. **Sugar** boosts browning at home-oven temps where Maillard is otherwise sluggish — and gives a hint of the slight sweetness that defines NY-style. **Oil** tenderizes the crumb, helps crust crispness, and slows staling. **8% levain over 48–72 h cold** is the DEAM lever: tiny inoculation, long enzymatic priming, big flavor for almost no work.

## The build

### 1. Pre-ferment wake-up (~1 h, 3 days before bake)

- In a large bowl: **water + levain + sugar + oil**. Stir until everything dissolves.
- Add **~150 g of the flour**. Stir to a thick batter. Cover.
- Wait ~1 h until you see active bubbling. Sugar will accelerate this — yeast loves easy fuel.

### 2. Final mix (10–15 min)

- Add the remaining flour and salt to the wake-up.
- Mix by hand or with a stand mixer hook on low for 6–8 min. Should be smooth, slightly tacky, and pull cleanly from the bowl.
- The dough will feel firmer than a typical sourdough dough at this hydration — that's correct.

### 3. Bulk + folds (~2 h at 22–24 °C)

- 1 stretch-and-fold at 30 min, then 1 light coil fold at 60 min.
- Done when the dough is smooth, has gained ~25–30% volume, and feels alive.

### 4. Ball and cold rest (48–72 h at 4 °C)

- Divide into **4 × 280 g pieces**. Ball each one tightly — this is critical. NY-style needs a ball with surface tension so it stretches into an even round later.
- Lightly oil each ball. Place in a sealed container with at least 2× headroom.
- **48–72 h in the fridge.**

> 48 h is good. **72 h is the sweet spot.** You'll lose some structure past 96 h as protease overruns the gluten — see [`../../theory/enzymes.md`](../../theory/enzymes.md). The long cold builds Maillard precursors that compensate for the cooler home-oven bake.

### 5. Final proof (1.5–3 h at room temp on bake day)

- Pull the balls out **1.5–3 h** before baking, depending on kitchen temp.
- They should look puffy and relaxed, jiggle slightly when you nudge the container.
- Slightly under-proofed is better than over for NY-style — you want the rim to puff in the oven.

### 6. Stretch

- Heavy dust of bench flour (50/50 bread flour + semolina works well).
- Slide a ball out gently. Press flat from center outward, leaving a ~1.5 cm rim untouched.
- Stretch hand-over-hand on the bench, or knuckles in the air, working larger gradually. **Aim for 14"** (~35 cm) — thinner in the middle (2–3 mm), slightly thicker rim.
- It's OK to use a rolling pin lightly if you can't get the size by hand. NY purists will yell at you. Tasty pizza will not.

### 7. Top

- **Sauce:** crushed San Marzano (or any good crushed tomato), salt, pinch of dried oregano, splash of olive oil. No need to cook it first — it'll cook on the pie.
- **Cheese:** low-moisture mozzarella (NOT fresh mozzarella — too wet for this style), shredded. Optionally a little provolone for tang. **Layer cheese first, then sauce in dollops on top** — the classic NY move keeps the cheese from getting soggy and lets the rim brown evenly.
- **No basil before bake.** Add fresh basil after if you want.

### 8. Bake (6–8 min at 260–290 °C)

- Preheat oven on **max** (usually 260–290 °C / 500–550 °F) with a stone or steel **on the upper-middle rack**, for at least **45 min**.
- Optional but excellent: switch to **broiler** for the last 2 min of preheating to push the stone temp up and scorch the cheese.
- Launch the pie on a peel dusted with semolina.
- **Bake 5–7 min** until the bottom is set and rim is starting to color.
- **Switch on broiler for the last 60–90 sec** to char the rim and brown the cheese. Watch it — don't walk away.
- Slide out, slice, eat. Wait 90 seconds before tearing in unless you want a roof-of-mouth burn.

> The broiler trick is the home-oven equivalent of the deck-oven char. If your oven has the broiler in the bottom drawer, just use top-rack with the broiler on at the end.

## Tuning levers

- **More color/char.** Longer broiler at the end. Or push the cold rest to the full 72 h for more residual sugars.
- **Crisper bottom.** Hotter stone (longer preheat). Thinner middle when stretching.
- **Chewier crumb.** Hold to 14% protein flour. Don't reduce hydration further.
- **More tang.** Push to 72+ h cold rest, or use a more mature/active levain.
- **Square Detroit/Sicilian variant.** Same dough, push to 70% hydration, drop into a heavily oiled pan, push out to fill, top edge-to-edge with cheese, bake at 260 °C for 12–15 min. The oil + pan does the bottom crisp.

## Troubleshooting

- **Pale, soft crust after bake.** Oven not hot enough, or skipped the broiler finish. Also: sugar/oil really do matter for NY-style browning.
- **Soggy center.** Too much sauce, fresh mozzarella instead of low-moisture, or rim never set (too cold a stone).
- **Dough won't hold a circle.** Under-proofed, or balls weren't shaped tightly enough — surface tension is everything.
- **Rim doesn't puff.** Over-proofed at room temp, or over-degassed during stretching. Be gentle with the rim.
- **Tough/leathery crust.** Over-baked, or the dough was over-fermented (slack and gluten-tired). Pull at full color, don't chase darker.

## Why DEAM works for NY style

NY-style was historically built on commercial yeast and a 1–3 day cold rest. The cold rest does two things: (1) builds flavor, (2) gives the gluten time to fully hydrate so the dough handles the long bake without going leathery. DEAM principles map cleanly:

- **Long cold rest** = exactly what NY-style was already doing, just done with sourdough levain instead of commercial yeast.
- **Low inoculation** (8%) lets the cold rest stretch to 72 h without over-fermentation.
- **Enzymatic priming** at 4 °C builds the maltose pool that browns the crust at home-oven temps where you don't have a 500 °C deck to bail you out.

See [`../methods/double-enzymatic-activation.md`](../methods/double-enzymatic-activation.md), and for why the long cold rest matters specifically for browning at lower temps, [`../../theory/flavor-chemistry.md`](../../theory/flavor-chemistry.md).
