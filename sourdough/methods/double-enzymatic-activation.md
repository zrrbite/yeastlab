# Double Enzymatic Activation Sourdough

A practical, low-maintenance approach to sourdough baking — designed for busy bakers who don't want to be tethered to daily levain feeds or tight production windows.

The method leverages the natural behavior of enzymes and microbes to build strength, extensibility, and flavor over time with minimal hands-on effort. By embracing a **cold-fed levain** and **staged enzymatic timing**, it comes close to the performance of traditional, high-effort workflows — without the overhead.

> **Core insight.** By understanding the timing of enzymatic activity — particularly amylase and protease — you can design your dough's schedule to work for you, not against you.
>
> In cold conditions, enzymes continue breaking down starch and protein even while yeast is dormant. This slow, passive *enzymatic priming* creates sugars for fermentation and gently softens the gluten network. By the time the yeast wakes up, the dough is extensible, sugar-rich, and ready to rise — with less hands-on input.

## TL;DR

- This is a **framework**, not a recipe. Plug in your favorite flour/water/salt ratios.
- You don't need to do everything in one go — build levain, build dough, bake can take days.
- **Early cold rest** lets amylase snip starch and release sugar. Schedule flexibility *and* a way to super-charge dough for fermentation.
- **Late cold rest** relaxes gluten for an open crumb.
- **Bulk in a wide container** — faster temperature equilibrium, more even fermentation, better gas distribution.

## What this method leverages

- **Enzymatic flavor prep at cold temps.** Amylase slowly converts starch into maltose while yeast rests, creating residual sugars for both Maillard browning and sweet, caramelized flavor.
- **Protease softening.** Controlled protease activity (pH-dependent) releases amino acids from gluten. These feed the Maillard reaction during baking, generating hundreds of volatile aroma compounds — the deep, toasty complexity of good sourdough: roasty, nutty, malty, buttery. Excess acid or enzyme activity tips this balance and causes gluten collapse.
- **LAB acidification control.** Balanced lactic/acetic profile builds acidity that enhances flavor without weakening structure.
- **Ester & diacetyl formation.** Ethanol (yeast) + organic acids (LAB) → fruity esters and buttery diacetyl, which give mature sourdough its rich aroma.
- **Temperature staging.** Alternating cold and warm phases optimizes enzyme action first, then yeast metabolism and flavor synthesis later.
- **Low inoculation rates.** Slower fermentation = longer enzyme window, more complex acid profile, better flavor layering.
- **Cold gluten hydration.** Early rest aligns gluten while enzymes work; extensibility improves without mechanical mixing. Less cleaning.
- **Convenience.** Cold phases act as fermentation pauses, letting flavor develop on your schedule.

For the full chemistry behind each of these, see [`../../theory/`](../../theory/).

## What makes this method different

Three key ideas:

1. **Prep levains for the entire week.** Feed once, stash in the fridge, develop slowly. Pull one out when you need it. The levain gets a boost by waking up in a pre-ferment built from your recipe's own ingredients.
2. **Enzymes work while yeast sleeps.** Controlling cold-rest timing builds flavor and dough extensibility passively — before bulk fermentation even begins.
3. **Low inoculation still gives high performance.** Less starter, better control, more flexibility. No flavor lost.
4. Plug in your favorite flour, water, salt.

## Overview: cold-fed levain workflow

Once or twice a week, feed and prep a few cold-fed levains in small jars:

- Fed 1:1:1 or 1:2:2
- Stored in the fridge for up to ~5 days

When you're ready to bake:

- Pull a levain from the fridge
- Wake it up with a pre-ferment built from your dough ingredients
- Mix into final dough → cold rest → bulk → bake

> **Example.** Feed 3 jars on Sunday. Use them across the week — try-hard loaf, discard loaf, pizza dough.

### Typical flow per bake

| Step                          | Timing                          |
|-------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| Cold-fed levain (from fridge) | Anytime                         |
| Pre-ferment wake-up           | ~6–8 hrs at room temp           |
| Final dough mix + cold rest   | Overnight (8–16 hrs)            |
| Bulk + folds                  | 3–4 hrs (day of bake)           |
| Shape, proof, bake            | Flexible (cold or warm proofing) |

### Folding strategy

- Stretch early for strength
- Coil (remaining) for extensibility

## The method, step by step

### 1. Prepare levain

On a flexible day (e.g. Sunday). See [`../starter.md`](../starter.md).

### 2. Bake day: grab a prepped levain from the fridge

- Pull one that's been in the fridge 1–5 days.
- No need to warm it up — you'll wake it up with the pre-ferment next.

> **Tip.** If the levain smells balanced (mildly tangy, bready) and hasn't collapsed into hooch, it's good to go. A little separation is fine.

How to tell if your starter is OK:

- Mild tang → OK
- Solvent / nail-polish smell → needs a feed
- Hooched but smells bready → OK with dilution
- Totally collapsed and harsh → revive with a feed or discard

### 3. Pre-ferment wake-up (morning of bake)

- Add flour per your recipe to one side of a bowl, water to the other side.
- Remove levain from the fridge and dissolve it in the water. Usual recipes call for 20%, but with this enzymatic approach, **10–15% suffices**.
- Mix a portion of the flour with the water/starter mixture until you have ~100% hydration (poolish-style preferment).
- Add the salt to the top of the flour mountain so you don't forget.
- Let it ferment for an hour or so, until you see bubbling / yeast waking up.
- Mix final dough using remaining flour and salt. The pre-ferment is wet enough to mix the salt easily.

### 4. Final dough mix + enzymatic rest

- Once mixed, place dough in the fridge for a long cold rest (~8–16 hrs).
- Protease is not yet very active because pH hasn't dropped (LAB haven't built much acid). Early cold rest doesn't degrade gluten — and you still have the option of a post-bulk cold rest later to relax gluten and improve structure.

This step allows:

- **Amylase** to break starch into sugars
- **Protease** to soften gluten very slightly (enhancing extensibility) at low activity

> **Insights.** The cold-fed levain is already sugar-rich from its fridge time. The pre-ferment acts like a metabolic reboot — yeast wakes into a nutrient-rich, low-stress environment. Enzymes continue working in the cold. You're not fermenting yet — you're prepping the dough for when the yeast wakes up.

### 5. Bulk fermentation & folding

- Remove dough from fridge and rest at room temp for ~30–60 min.
- Folding regimen — anything you like. Suggested:
  - 1 early stretch-and-fold to build foundational structure
  - 3–4 gentle coil folds spaced 45 min – 1 hr apart
- **Bulk time may be shorter than usual** because the dough is already enzymatically primed. A test bake came in at 3 hrs of bulk at 75 °F after 12 hrs in the fridge.

> **Insight.** Early folds develop strength, while coil folds maintain structure without degassing. You're guiding the dough, not wrestling it. Bulk doesn't need to go 7–8 hours. Sugar-primed dough at 22–24 °C with 10–20% inoculation may need only 3–5 hours. Watch the dough.

### 6. Shape, rest, bake

- Do what you want with the dough.
- If you're cold-proofing in a banneton, consider shortening the earlier cold rest slightly to avoid over-softening the gluten.
- Common move: shape, rest a bit more in the fridge, then bake (e.g. baguettes).

## Summary

- **Cold-fed levain** — enzyme activation while yeast sleeps.
- **Enzyme-driven rest phases** — build dough flavor + structure before bulk.
- **Pre-ferment wake-up** — quick, balanced rise from a primed starter.
- **Cold dough rest** — second enzymatic phase (extensibility + flavor).
- **Low inoculation** — fewer ingredients used, longer flavor window.

You prep levains once a week instead of feeding daily. You let enzymes do the work during the day while you're at work. You bake in the evening without watching a clock. You waste less, discard less, and bake more consistently.

### Open questions / experiments

- Use cold fermentation **before** bulk and **before** pH drops, letting amylase convert starch to sugar without protease attacking gluten.
- Use the fridge as much as you want, but mind that the later in the process, the more active protease becomes.
- A more mature starter with more LABs could generate more flavor in a powerful-fermentation environment. Organic acids + yeast byproducts = esters = flavor. But this could mean less diacetyl?
- Diacetyl is a byproduct of sugar metabolism (citrate or glucose breakdown). Long fermentation and low pH degrade diacetyl. So perhaps a long early cold rest creates lots of diacetyl, then shorter bulk preserves it. Try: warm start to let LAB build diacetyl before pH drops, then cool down to slow LAB and acid.
- Possible rhythm: **mix → cold rest (sugars) → warm bulk 25–28 °C (LAB build, citrate → diacetyl) → cold rest (slow LABs) → bulk again → another cold rest**.

## Think like a builder

Before you start, talk to yourself in **baker's percentages**. Think like a builder, not a follower. Your formula is a starting ratio, not a rulebook. Adjust water, flour types, and fermentation timing to match your goals.

Flour = 100%, all other ingredients are percentages relative to flour.

| Ingredient        | Typical %  | Notes                                                |
|-------------------|-----------:|------------------------------------------------------|
| Flour             | 100%       | Always the base                                      |
| Water             | 60–85%     | More water = more open crumb, more extensibility     |
| Levain / starter  | 10–30%     | Less for longer ferments; more for speed or control  |
| Salt              | 1.8–2.2%   | More salt tightens gluten and slows fermentation     |
| Whole wheat / rye | 0–100%     | More flavor, nutrients, enzymatic activity           |

Refine to your goals:

| Goal                  | Adjustments                                                              |
|-----------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Open crumb            | Raise hydration to 75–80%+                                               |
| Complex flavor        | Add 10–30% whole wheat / spelt; use a more mature starter                |
| Speed up              | Increase starter %, use warmer bulk                                      |
| Slow down / cold rest | Lower starter to 10–15% and chill after mix                              |
| Tighter structure     | Lower hydration, mix more, fold often                                    |
| Extended shelf life   | Whole grains + slightly higher salt + long cold ferment                  |

## Tuning levers

**Want a sweeter crust?**

- Lengthen the early cold rest (more amylase → more residual sugar).
- Shorten bulk (less sugar consumption by yeast).
- Bake hot with steam.

**Want a tangier crumb?**

- Use a stiffer levain (50–65% hydration) and lengthen the cold proof.
- Lower inoculation, push fermentation longer.
- Slightly higher salt.

**Want buttery / diacetyl notes?**

- Warm early phase (25–28 °C) for ~4–6 hrs to let LAB build diacetyl before pH drops below ~4.0.
- Don't store the levain cold for *too* long — diacetyl degrades over time.

**Want fruity esters?**

- Wet preferments (100% hydration).
- Warm fermentation phase ~20–25 °C.
- Avoid overly acidic environments — low pH destroys esters.

**Want it same-day?**

- Skip the long cold rest. Use a fully woken levain, slightly higher inoculation (15–20%), bulk at 26–28 °C. You lose some flavor depth but gain speed.

## Reference: full method overview

| Phase              | Temp        | Duration   | Focus                                       |
|--------------------|-------------|-----------:|---------------------------------------------|
| Enzymatic pre-phase | 4–6 °C      | 8–12 h     | Sugar & amino-acid buildup                  |
| Warm activation    | 26–28 °C    | ~1 h       | Yeast/LAB kick-off, early esters            |
| Controlled bulk    | 22–24 °C    | 2–3 h      | Structure + balance                         |
| Cold proof         | 4 °C        | 8–18 h     | Flavor integration, structure hold          |
| Bake               | 230–250 °C  | until full color | Maillard + caramelization              |

Conceptually:

> Cold start = enzymes first → warm pulse = microbes bloom → cold finish = lock flavor in place.
