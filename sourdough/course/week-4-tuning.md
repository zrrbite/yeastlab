# Week 4 — Tuning + framework

You've baked twice. You've got a healthy starter. You've used the DEAM weekly rhythm. **Now stop following recipes and start designing them.**

This week is about three things:

1. **Baker's percentages** — the simple math that makes recipes flexible
2. **Tuning levers** — how to push a bake toward sweet, tangy, buttery, or fruity
3. **An intentional bake** — apply a chosen tuning lever and observe the difference

By the end of week 4, you should be able to look at any recipe and **ask "why is the hydration that high?", "why that much salt?", "what would happen if I cold-rested longer?"** — and answer those questions yourself.

## Baker's percentages — the simple math

Here's the entire concept:

> **Flour is 100%. Everything else is a percentage of the flour.**

That's it. Bakers express recipes as ratios because **ratios scale**.

### Example: your week 3 boule, in baker's percentages

You used:

- **500 g flour** → 100%
- **350 g water** → 70%
- **9 g salt** → 1.8%
- **125 g levain** → 25%

Now if you want to make twice as much bread:

- **1000 g flour** → 100%
- **700 g water** → 70%
- **18 g salt** → 1.8%
- **250 g levain** → 25%

The proportions stay the same; the absolute amounts scale. That's why bakers think this way.

### The standard ranges

For sourdough bread:

| Ingredient            | Bakers % | What it does                                                    |
|-----------------------|----------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Flour                 | 100%     | The base. Always.                                               |
| Water (hydration)     | 60–85%   | Higher = wetter dough, more open crumb, harder to handle        |
| Salt                  | 1.8–2.2% | Below 1.8% = bland and over-fermented; above 2.2% = tight & slow |
| Levain                | 10–30%   | More = faster ferment; less = slower, more flavor depth          |
| Whole wheat or rye    | 0–100%   | More = more enzyme activity, deeper flavor, denser crumb         |

These are the **levers** in every sourdough recipe.

### Try this exercise

Look at the focaccia recipe in [`week-2-first-bake.md`](week-2-first-bake.md). Calculate its baker's percentages. (Hint: 400 g flour, 300 g water, 8 g salt, 80 g starter.) What's the hydration? Salt %? Inoculation %?

Now look at the boule from week 3. Same exercise. Compare them.

You'll see they're **roughly similar** but tuned differently — focaccia is a touch wetter, lower inoculation, simpler. The boule is more developed.

This is how recipes work. Once you see them as ratios, you can adjust them.

## Tuning levers — what each one shifts

There are five big knobs you can turn in DEAM. Each shifts the final bread in a known direction.

### 1. Hydration

**Lever range:** 60–85% water

| Direction        | Effect                                                              |
|------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Lower (60–70%)   | Tighter crumb, easier to shape, sturdier slice. Sandwich loaves.    |
| Higher (75–85%)  | More open crumb, harder to handle, dramatic-looking loaf. Country breads. |

For a beginner: **65–72%** is the sweet spot. Move higher as your dough handling improves.

### 2. Inoculation (% levain)

**Lever range:** 10–30%

| Direction        | Effect                                                              |
|------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Lower (10–15%)   | Slower ferment, more time for amylase + LAB, deeper flavor          |
| Higher (20–30%)  | Faster ferment, less flavor depth, easier same-day baking           |

For DEAM specifically: **lower inoculation (10–15%) works** because the cold rest does the priming. This is the DEAM superpower — flavor without the time penalty.

### 3. Cold rest length

**Lever range:** 4–24 hours at 4 °C

| Direction        | Effect                                                              |
|------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Shorter (4–8 hr) | Less sugar liberated, less flavor development, fewer schedule problems |
| Longer (12–24 hr)| More sugar = sweeter crust, more flavor, deeper crumb. Risk: protease starts to soften gluten if too long |

For week 4: **try a longer cold rest** (16–24 hours). See what happens.

### 4. Bulk fermentation temperature

**Lever range:** 18–28 °C

| Direction        | Effect                                                              |
|------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Cooler (18–22 °C)| Slower bulk, more acetic acid (sharper tang), tighter timeline      |
| Warmer (24–28 °C)| Faster bulk, more lactic acid (smoother sourness), more diacetyl (buttery) |

A cool kitchen (winter) and a warm kitchen (summer) **bake different breads** even from the same recipe. Pay attention.

### 5. Whole grain %

**Lever range:** 0–30% (in the dough; can go higher but flavor changes a lot)

| Direction        | Effect                                                              |
|------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 0% (all white)   | Cleanest crumb, lightest flavor                                     |
| 5–15%            | Subtle nutty/wheat character, faster ferment, deeper crust          |
| 20–30%           | Pronounced whole-grain flavor, denser crumb, much faster ferment    |

For week 4 experiment: try **10–15% whole wheat in the final dough** (not just the levain). See how it changes the bread.

## Picking a tuning experiment

Pick **one** of the following experiments for your week 4 bake. Don't change multiple variables at once — you won't know what caused the change.

### Experiment A: Sweeter crust

Push the early cold rest to **18–24 hours**. More amylase activity → more residual sugar at bake time → more Maillard browning → sweeter, deeper crust.

### Experiment B: Tangier crumb

Lower inoculation to **10%**, push the cold rest to **16 hours**, do a final cold proof of **18 hours**. More fermentation time + more LAB activity → more sourness.

### Experiment C: Buttery / diacetyl notes

Bulk at **26–28 °C** for the first few hours (place dough near a warm spot — top of fridge, or in oven with light on). LAB will produce diacetyl in the warm phase. Then crash it cold for proof.

### Experiment D: More whole-grain depth

Add **15% whole wheat** to the final dough (so for 500 g total flour: 425 g bread flour + 75 g whole wheat). Observe denser crumb and deeper flavor.

### Experiment E: Higher hydration

Push to **75% hydration**. More water = more open crumb potential. **Harder to handle** — be prepared with wet hands and a bench scraper.

Pick the one that excites you. **Bake using the same week 3 process**, but with the tuning lever applied.

## What to do during the bake

Pay attention to:

- **How does the dough feel different** at the end of bulk?
- **What does it smell like** at each stage?
- **What does the bake look like** compared to week 3?
- **What does the crumb look like** when you slice?
- **What does it taste like?**

Take notes. Compare to your week 3 boule.

## Reading other recipes

Now go look at any sourdough recipe online. You should be able to **decode it** in seconds:

- "What's the hydration?"
- "What's the inoculation?"
- "What kind of fermentation timing?"
- "Is this a long-cold-rest method like DEAM, or a same-day method?"

You'll start seeing recipes as **points on a knob-space** rather than fixed instructions. That's the unlock.

## When to break the rules

The percentages in this file are **typical ranges** — not laws. You can make great bread outside of them. Once you understand what each lever does, you can:

- Bake at 90% hydration if you have the skill
- Push inoculation to 5% for very long, very flavorful ferments
- Mash 100% rye for dense rye bread
- Skip the cold rest entirely for a fast bake

Each choice is a trade-off. The framework gives you a way to **think about** those trade-offs instead of guessing.
