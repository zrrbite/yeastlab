# Week 0 — Prerequisites

Before you start the course, you need a few things. None are expensive; most you probably already have.

## Equipment — essential

### A digital kitchen scale (1 g resolution)

**The single most important purchase.** Sourdough is measured by weight, not volume. "1 cup of flour" is anywhere from 120 g to 180 g depending on how packed it is. **You cannot make consistent sourdough with cups.**

A basic ~$15 scale that reads in grams to 1 g resolution is enough. Look for:

- Tare function (zeros out the weight of the bowl)
- Reads in grams (not just ounces)
- Holds 5+ kg max weight

If you're going to buy one piece of equipment for this course, this is it.

### Mixing bowl

Anything large enough to mix 1 kg of flour + water + starter. A **medium-large stainless or glass bowl** works. Plastic is fine.

### A jar for the starter

A 500 mL (~16 oz) glass jar with a loose-fitting lid. Mason jars are perfect. **Don't seal it tight** — fermentation produces CO₂ that needs to escape. A loose lid or a piece of cloth + rubber band works.

### Something to bake in

Any of these works:

- **Dutch oven** (4–6 qt with lid) — best for boules, traps steam, gives oven spring. Worth getting eventually but not for week 2.
- **Sheet pan** — for focaccia (week 2). You almost certainly have one.
- **Cast-iron skillet + roasting pan inverted on top** — DIY Dutch oven for a boule.
- **Baking stone or steel + oven steam tray** — flatbreads, baguettes.

For the course: a **standard sheet pan or baking dish (like a 9x13 pyrex)** for week 2's focaccia is enough.

### Oven

Your normal kitchen oven. Doesn't need to be fancy. **Most ovens lie about their temperature** — by week 4 you'll wish you had an oven thermometer ($10) to know your real temps.

## Equipment — nice to have

You don't need these for the course but they make things easier:

- **Bench scraper** (~$5) — handles wet dough way better than your hands
- **Instant-read thermometer** (~$10–$20) — for measuring water temp and dough temp; useful but not essential
- **Banneton / proofing basket** (~$15) — gives a shaped boule its classic ribs and structure. Not needed for focaccia.
- **Razor blade or scoring lame** ($5) — for scoring the top of boules; not needed for focaccia.
- **Plastic dough container** with markings (Cambro 6 qt or similar) — lets you see when dough has doubled. Useful in week 3+.
- **Oven thermometer** ($10) — knows what temp your oven actually runs at
- **Pizza peel** or thin cutting board — for sliding loaves into the oven

Don't spend more than $50 total to start. You can add tools as you discover you want them.

## Flour

Two flours, that's it for the whole course:

### A 5 lb bag of bread flour

**"Bread flour" or "high-protein flour"** — typically 12–13% protein. Examples in the US:

- King Arthur Bread Flour (the standard, available everywhere)
- Bob's Red Mill Bread Flour
- Generic store-brand bread flour

**Avoid:** all-purpose flour (lower protein → weaker dough), pastry flour, self-rising flour, gluten-free flour.

If you can only get all-purpose, it'll work but the dough will be slightly weaker. King Arthur AP is high enough protein (~11.7%) to be usable.

### A small bag of whole wheat or rye flour

A 1 lb bag. You'll use **tiny amounts** — 5–25 g per feeding — as "super food" for your starter. The whole-grain bran is full of enzymes and nutrients that wild yeast and LAB love.

**Either works:**

- **Whole wheat** — easier to find, milder flavor
- **Rye** — slightly more enzymatic, common preference for starter feeding

Doesn't matter much which. Get the smaller bag — you won't go through it fast.

## Water

For most US/EU tap water, **just use tap water.** It's fine.

The only concern is **chlorine**:

- If your tap water tastes/smells of chlorine, leave it in an open jar overnight to off-gas, OR use bottled spring water for the first few days of building your starter.
- After the starter is established, mild chlorine doesn't hurt much.

**Don't use distilled water** for sourdough — there are no minerals, and yeast/LAB need them. Tap or spring water is correct.

If you live somewhere with **very hard water** (mineral-rich) or **very soft water** (almost no minerals), you may eventually want to think about water composition — see [`../../theory/water-minerals.md`](../../theory/water-minerals.md) — but **don't worry about it for the course**. Tap water is fine.

## A few other consumables

- **Salt** — fine sea salt or kosher salt. Avoid iodized table salt (the iodine can mess with starters at high concentrations, though modern thinking is that it's fine in normal amounts; just easier to avoid).
- **Olive oil** — for the focaccia in week 2. Any decent olive oil works.
- **Parchment paper** — useful for transfers and bake liners. Optional but handy.

## Mindset

Three things to keep in mind before you start:

### 1. Sourdough is a relationship with a culture

Your starter is **alive**. It's a community of wild yeast and bacteria that you're cultivating. It needs feeding, attention, and warmth. It also forgives mistakes — within reason. You're not making a recipe; you're tending a small living ecosystem.

### 2. The first few bakes will be imperfect

Probably ugly. Possibly dense. Maybe weirdly shaped. **Bake them anyway. Eat them.** A failed sourdough loaf is still bread, and you learn more from one imperfect bake than from reading 50 articles.

### 3. The schedule should serve you, not the other way around

DEAM is built for real lives — work, kids, weekends. You shouldn't be setting alarms for 3 AM to fold dough. If a step doesn't fit your day, **shift it cold** (slows everything down) or **adjust temperature**. The method is flexible. Trust that and use it.
