# Week 2 — First bake: focaccia

This week you bake your first sourdough loaf. We're starting with **focaccia** because:

- **No shaping.** Focaccia is poured into a pan; no gluten gymnastics.
- **No scoring.** No razor blade, no decorative cuts, no oven-spring obsession.
- **Forgiving hydration.** Focaccia is wet — but in a way that produces soft, pillowy crumb, not a sticky disaster.
- **Fast feedback.** You can taste the result the same day you bake.

This is the bake that builds **confidence**. We'll do baguettes and boules later when you have intuition.

## What you'll need

- **Your starter from week 1** (out of the fridge the night before)
- **400 g bread flour**
- **300 g water** (room temperature)
- **8 g salt** (~1.5 tsp)
- **80 g cold-fed starter** (about 1/3 cup of starter, depending on hydration)
- **Olive oil** (for the pan and the top — generous)
- **Optional toppings:** fresh rosemary, flaky sea salt, halved cherry tomatoes, sliced garlic, olives

Total time: **~3 hours of active steps spread across 18–24 hours**, but most of that is dough sitting and doing its own thing.

## The day before bake — wake up your starter

If your starter is in the fridge, you need to wake it up.

The night before bake (so 18–24 hours before you want focaccia):

1. **Take the starter out of the fridge.**
2. **Discard all but 25 g.**
3. **Feed it 50 g flour + 50 g water** (a 1:2:2 ratio — gives it food to work with).
4. **Cover, leave on counter overnight.**

By morning the starter should be **active, bubbly, and risen** — back to the state you had on Day 7 of week 1. This is what you'll bake with.

If your kitchen is cold and the starter looks sluggish in the morning, give it 2–4 more hours at room temp before mixing the dough.

## Bake day — morning: pre-ferment

Around mid-morning, when your starter is clearly active:

1. **Get a large mixing bowl.**
2. **Add the 300 g water.**
3. **Add 80 g of your active starter** to the water. Stir until it dissolves (some clumps are fine).
4. **Add 200 g of the bread flour** (about half). Stir to a thick batter.
5. **In a separate bowl**, mix the remaining 200 g flour with the 8 g salt.
6. **Cover the wet mix.** Let it sit **45 minutes – 1 hour.**

This is the "pre-ferment wake-up." You're letting the yeast in your starter feast on the new flour and water in a low-stress environment. By the end of an hour you should see **bubbles forming on the surface** of the wet mixture.

> **What's happening:** the yeast is multiplying rapidly in the new food source. The amylase in the flour is starting to release sugars. By the time you add the rest of the flour, the dough will already be primed for fermentation.

## Bake day — late morning: mix final dough

After the pre-ferment hour:

1. **Add the salted flour mix** to the wet starter mix.
2. **Mix with your hand** (or a spatula). You want a **rough, shaggy dough** — no dry flour patches, but don't overdo it.
3. **Cover. Let it rest 30 minutes.**

This rest (called an "autolyse" by purists) lets the flour fully hydrate without you having to knead. Less work, better dough.

## Bake day — early afternoon: stretch and fold

After the 30-minute rest, do **one round of stretch-and-folds**:

1. **Wet your hand** (prevents sticking).
2. **Reach under one side of the dough**, lift it up, and fold it over the top.
3. **Rotate the bowl 90°** and do the same on the next side.
4. **Repeat on the other two sides.** That's one round = 4 folds.

The dough should already feel **stronger and smoother** than when you mixed it. That's the magic of autolyse.

**Cover. Let it rest 1 hour** at room temperature.

## Bake day — afternoon: into the fridge

After the 1-hour rest:

1. **Lightly oil** a sheet pan or 9x13 dish (1–2 tbsp olive oil, spread to cover).
2. **Pour the dough into the pan.** It will be soft and probably won't fill the pan yet — that's fine.
3. **Drizzle a little more oil over the top.** Cover with plastic wrap or a lid.
4. **Put it in the fridge** for **4–8 hours** (overnight is fine if you want to bake tomorrow morning).

This cold rest is the **enzymatic phase** — the heart of DEAM. Amylase is breaking down starch into sugar; the dough is developing flavor and extensibility while the yeast slows down. You're not trying to ferment now; you're priming.

> **Schedule flexibility:** if you mixed in the morning, you could bake tonight. If you mixed in the afternoon, plan to bake tomorrow morning. The cold rest can stretch from 4 hours up to 16 hours without major issues.

## Bake day — evening (or next morning): final shape and proof

When you're ready to bake:

1. **Take the dough out of the fridge.**
2. **Let it warm up in the pan** for **45–60 minutes**. Just sit on the counter.
3. **Wet or oil your fingertips.**
4. **"Dimple" the dough** by pressing your fingertips into it across the entire surface. Push them all the way down to the bottom of the pan. Don't worry about deflating — focaccia wants those gas pockets to spread.
5. **The dough should now fill the pan** more or less. If not, gently tug it toward the edges.
6. **Drizzle generously** with olive oil — be liberal. 2–3 tbsp.
7. **Sprinkle flaky salt** all over.
8. **Add toppings** if using — push them gently into the dough so they don't burn.

Let it sit **another 30 minutes** while the oven preheats.

## Bake

1. **Preheat oven** to **240 °C / 465 °F** (or as hot as your oven safely goes for bread baking).
2. **Bake 20–25 minutes**, until **deeply golden brown** on top.
3. The bottom should be **crisp** — lift a corner with a spatula to check.

When it looks like the picture in your head of "good focaccia," it's done.

## Cool, slice, eat

1. **Let it cool 10 minutes** in the pan.
2. **Slide it out** onto a cutting board.
3. **Slice into squares.**
4. **Eat warm.**

## What to expect from your first bake

It will probably be:

- **Less puffed than store-bought focaccia.** Beginner doughs don't get as much oven spring. Normal.
- **Maybe slightly dense.** Same reason. Normal.
- **Less sour than you might expect.** A young 1–2 week starter doesn't have much LAB development yet. The sourness builds over weeks of starter maturity.
- **Tasty.** Olive oil, salt, fresh bread, warm. It will be good.

Take a photo. Eat half. Save the rest in a paper bag (not plastic — sweats) for the next 1–2 days.

## What to log

In your notebook (you started a notebook, right?), write down:

- Date and time you woke the starter
- Date and time you mixed dough
- Length of cold rest (hours)
- Final dough temp before baking (if you have a thermometer)
- Bake time
- What you observed at each stage (smell, look, feel of dough)
- How it tasted
- One thing you'd do differently

This is the start of your personal DEAM playbook. By bake 5 it'll be more useful than any recipe.

## Common first-bake issues

### "It didn't rise much"

Most likely: starter wasn't active enough, OR the dough was too cold for too long. Next time:

- Make sure starter is **clearly bubbly and risen** before you use it
- Let dough warm up longer after the cold rest (60–90 min instead of 45)

### "It's gummy in the middle"

Underbaked. Bake longer next time, or hotter.

### "It's tough / not soft"

Probably overbaked, OR your hydration was off (too dry). Check that you weighed water accurately.

### "It collapsed when I dimpled it"

Either over-proofed (too long at room temp before dimpling) or under-developed gluten (dough too weak). Next time, dimple **earlier** before it gets too gassy.

### "It tastes bland"

Young starter. This will improve. Add more topping ideas next time (rosemary + garlic + tomato is a great combo).

### "The bottom burned"

Your oven runs hot. Use a lower rack next time, or lower the temp by 20 °C.
