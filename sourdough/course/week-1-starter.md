# Week 1 — Build a starter

This week you turn flour and water into a living sourdough culture. By Day 7 you'll have something **bubbly, sweet-smelling, and alive** — ready to bake with next week.

## What's actually happening

You're not "making yeast." Wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are **already living on the surface of flour, in the air, on your hands.** When you mix flour with water and leave it warm, they wake up, eat the sugars, and reproduce. Within a week, you have a stable community.

The rough timeline:

- **Days 1–2:** random microbes wake up. Smells weird (vegetal, sometimes like wet sock). This is normal — it's the "stink phase."
- **Days 3–4:** LAB take over. pH drops. Smells acidic, slightly fruity. The stink fades.
- **Days 5–7:** yeast catches up to LAB. Bubbles increase. The starter starts to **rise visibly** between feeds.
- **Day 7+:** stable enough to bake with. **Full maturity (max flavor potential) takes 3–4 weeks**, but it's bakeable now.

You don't need to understand the chemistry to make a starter. But if you want the deeper view, see [`../starter.md`](../starter.md) and [`../../theory/fermentation.md`](../../theory/fermentation.md).

## Daily ritual

Same thing every day at roughly the same time:

1. Look at the starter
2. Give it a stir
3. Discard most of it
4. Add fresh flour + water in equal parts
5. Stir well, cover loosely, leave on counter

Takes about 5 minutes. That's the whole week 1 commitment.

## Day-by-day

### Day 1 — Mix

In your jar:

- **50 g bread flour**
- **5 g whole wheat or rye flour** (the "super food")
- **55 g water** (slightly warm — body temperature, ~25–30 °C / 78–86 °F)

Stir vigorously with a fork or spoon for 30 seconds. Should look like thick pancake batter.

- **Cover loosely** (lid sitting on top, not screwed down; or cloth + rubber band).
- **Leave on the counter** in a normal-temperature spot (~20–25 °C / 68–77 °F).
- **Mark the level** of the mixture on the side of the jar with a rubber band or a sharpie line. This lets you see if it rises.

That's day 1.

### Day 2 — Probably nothing visible

Look at it. Probably no bubbles. Probably smells slightly off — vegetal, like wet leaves or socks. **This is correct.**

Stir it briefly with a clean spoon (introduces some oxygen, encourages yeast).

**Today: don't feed.** Just stir, observe, leave alone.

### Day 3 — First feed

Today you start feeding. The pattern: **discard most, then refresh.**

1. **Stir the starter.**
2. **Discard all but ~25 g.** (Pour or scoop most of it out — into the trash, compost, or the discard jar in your fridge.)
3. **Add 25 g bread flour + 25 g water.** (Skip the rye/wheat today — your starter will get plenty in subsequent feeds.)
4. Stir well. Mark new level. Cover loosely.

By now you might see **a few small bubbles**. The smell may have shifted from vegetal to mildly fruity or beery. Both are good signs.

Don't worry if there are no visible signs yet — wild yeast populations are still building.

### Day 4 — Feed

Same as Day 3:

1. Stir
2. Discard all but ~25 g
3. Add 25 g bread flour + 25 g water + a pinch (~3 g) of whole wheat/rye
4. Mark level

**You should see bubbles by now.** Maybe small, maybe scattered. If yes, you're on track. If no, your kitchen might be cold — see "troubleshooting" below.

The smell should be **noticeably different** from day 1 — less weird, more bread-adjacent.

### Day 5 — Feed; possibly visible rise

Same routine. By now your starter should be **rising slightly** between feeds — maybe 25–50% increase in volume during the 24-hour window.

If you're seeing real rise + bubbles + a pleasant slightly-sour smell: **you're doing well.** The yeast is established.

Today, also **stir the starter once 12 hours after the morning feed**. (So feed at 8 AM, stir at 8 PM.) This adds oxygen, accelerates yeast growth.

### Day 6 — Feed; should be rising visibly

Same routine. By now, expect:

- **Rises noticeably** (50–100% in 24 hours)
- **Lots of bubbles** throughout
- **Pleasant aroma** — slightly sour, slightly sweet, maybe a bit yogurty or fruity
- Texture is **gloopy and aerated**, not dense

If yes — you're ready for the next step.

### Day 7 — Feed in the morning; observe peak

Today, feed in the morning. Mark the level.

**Watch what happens over the day.** Your starter should:

- Rise by 50–100%+ (sometimes much more)
- Reach a **peak** sometime in the afternoon (4–8 hours after feeding)
- Hold the peak briefly
- Start to **fall back** as the yeast runs out of food

That peak-and-fall behavior is what you want. **A starter at peak is at maximum lift potential.**

If your starter does this on Day 7 — **congrats, your starter is alive and bakeable.**

If it's still sluggish, give it 2–3 more days of the same routine. Some starters take 10–14 days to fully establish, especially in cool kitchens.

## What "ready to bake with" looks like

Your starter is ready when:

- It **doubles** (or close to it) within 6–8 hours of feeding
- It **smells balanced** — pleasantly sour, slightly fruity or yogurty, not vinegary or solvent-like
- It has **lots of bubbles** throughout, not just on top
- The **float test passes** (optional but classic): drop a small spoonful of recently-peaked starter into a glass of water. If it floats, the gas inside is enough to lift dough.

If yes to all of these: **you're ready for week 2.** If not, give it more time.

## Storing your starter going forward

After day 7 (or whenever it's stable), you have options:

### Option A — Keep on the counter (high maintenance)

Feed once or twice daily. Always at peak when you want to bake. Most flexible but most demanding. Most beginners try this first and burn out.

### Option B — Move to the fridge (low maintenance — recommended for DEAM)

Once your starter is stable:

1. Feed it normally
2. Wait 4–6 hours (until it's clearly active and rising)
3. **Move the jar to the fridge**

In the fridge, **fermentation slows ~10×.** A starter that needed daily feeding at room temp now needs **once-a-week feeding** in the fridge.

This is the DEAM strategy. You'll set this up properly in week 3.

For now (between week 1 and week 2): **move your starter to the fridge after Day 7's feed.** Take it out of the fridge the night before you bake in week 2.

## Common week 1 issues

### "It smells terrible"

If it smells like cheese, vomit, or rotten food at any point in days 1–3 — **that's normal, just wait it out.** It's the early bacteria phase.

If it still smells terrible by day 5 — start over with fresh flour. Sometimes a starter just doesn't take off and it's faster to restart.

### "No bubbles, no rise after 5 days"

Most likely: your kitchen is cold. Wild yeast wakes up slowly below 20 °C / 68 °F.

Try:

- Move the jar to a warmer spot (top of the fridge, near a sunny window, in an oven with the light on)
- Add a tiny bit more rye/whole wheat at the next feed
- Switch to slightly warmer water (~30 °C / 86 °F)
- Be patient — week 2 instead of week 1 is fine

### "It rose huge then collapsed"

That's actually a sign of healthy yeast. It rose to peak then fell back when food ran out. You probably need to **feed it sooner** next time — or use a higher feeding ratio (more flour and water relative to existing starter).

### "Hooch" (gray liquid on top)

Brown/gray liquid means the starter is hungry — it's the alcohol byproduct of late-stage fermentation. **Just stir it back in and feed.** Hooch is fine, not bad.

### "There's mold"

Pink, green, blue, or fuzzy growth on top = throw it out and start over. (Black-gray hooch isn't mold; it's the alcohol. Mold has texture and color.)

This is rare in established starters but possible if a non-yeast microbe got established early.

## A small ritual to consider

Many people **name their starter.** Sounds silly, but you'll find it helps you remember to feed it consistently. "Did I feed Bartholomew today?" is a sentence that gets bread baked.
