# Next steps — after the course

You finished. You have a working starter, a weekly DEAM rhythm, three bakes, and the framework. **Where now?**

A few directions, ordered by what most people will want.

## Bake more

The best thing you can do is **just keep baking**. Pattern recognition comes from repetition, not from reading more. Aim for **one bake every 1–2 weeks** for the next few months.

A reasonable progression:

1. **Repeat the boule** with the same parameters 2–3 times. Notice what's the same and what's different bake to bake. Build muscle memory.
2. **Try a new tuning lever** each bake. Vary one thing, see what changes.
3. **Try a new shape:** batard (oval) before baguettes (long thin).
4. **Try a new recipe** from [`../recipes/`](../recipes/).

### Books

If you want a single book to deepen your sourdough practice:

- **Ken Forkish — *Flour Water Salt Yeast*** (2012). The accessible introduction; uses commercial yeast for some recipes but the sourdough section is solid.
- **Chad Robertson — *Tartine Bread*** (2010). The foundational modern sourdough book. Beautiful, inspiring, sometimes vague on specifics.
- **Trevor J. Wilson — *Open Crumb Mastery*** (2018). Very specific, focused on the open-crumb obsession; deeper than the average book.
- **Ed Crocker (Hank Wilson) — *The Bread Baker's Apprentice*** (2001). Classic reference, covers many breads beyond sourdough.

## Improve specific skills

### Shaping

The single biggest skill leap from "OK loaves" to "good loaves" is **better shaping**. Shaping is about creating **surface tension** that lets the loaf hold its form during proof and spring during bake.

Best resource: **YouTube. Search "boule shaping technique" or "batard shaping" and watch 5 different bakers**. You'll see different approaches; pick what makes sense for your hands.

Practice **just shaping** — even on dough that's not destined for a real bake. Mix a small amount of dough, do a stretch-and-fold, shape, watch what happens to the dough's structure.

### Scoring

A separate skill from shaping. Read about it, watch videos, practice. The "ear" comes from:

- A sharp blade (replaceable lame, or razor blade in a holder)
- A 30–45° angle (not perpendicular)
- A single confident slash, not multiple cautious cuts
- Underproofing very slightly so there's energy left to push through the score

### Hydration

Slowly push your hydration **up over the months**. Your week 3 boule was probably ~70%. Try 73%, then 75%, then 78%. Each step requires more handling skill.

### Timing intuition

By bake 10, you should be able to **look at a dough at the end of bulk and know if it's done**. Without a timer. Without a scale. Without checking notes.

That's the skill that takes time and practice to develop, and it's the one that turns sourdough from "following a recipe" into "tending to a dough."

## Branch out

Once you're comfortable with sourdough, the chemistry transfers:

### Other ferments to try

- **Pizza dough** with commercial yeast (faster, simpler, great date-night recipe). See [`../../bakers-yeast/`](../../bakers-yeast/) (placeholder for now, but the principle is the same as DEAM but faster).
- **Fresh pasta** — different texture, no fermentation, but flour-water-salt gluten development is the same skill set.
- **Brewing** — beer, hard cider, mead, wine. The yeast/LAB chemistry you learned in sourdough applies directly. See [`../../beer/`](../../beer/).
- **Coffee** — yes, coffee. The fermentation in coffee processing is the same family of microbes as sourdough. See [`../../coffee/`](../../coffee/).

### Going pro / serious hobby

If you fall in love with this:

- **Bread bench at a farmers' market** — surprisingly accessible, especially with sourdough's specialty positioning.
- **Bread CSA / subscription** — bake for friends and neighbors on a fixed weekly schedule.
- **Bread events / pop-ups** — lots of cities have bread-curious crowds.
- **Pursue a deeper recipe library** — try every classic European bread, then every classic American.

## Maintain your starter long-term

A few practical notes for after the course:

- **Keep it in the fridge between bakes.** Once a week max for active feeding if you're baking weekly.
- **Refresh it once a week even if you don't bake.** Just feed and put back in the fridge. Doesn't take 5 minutes.
- **Travel-prep:** if you're going away for 2+ weeks, feed it the day before, then leave in the fridge. It'll be fine.
- **For longer than 2 months:** consider drying some starter on parchment paper as backup. Crumble, store in an airtight container indefinitely. Rehydrate when you come back.
- **Naming and dating:** if you have multiple starters or split your starter, label clearly. A starter is yours; it has a lineage.

## A final thought

**Sourdough is a slow craft.** Three years in, you'll be making bread better than you can imagine making now. Five years in, you'll have intuitions you can't explain. **Don't expect mastery in 4 weeks** — expect to start seeing the shape of mastery, and to enjoy the bread along the way.

The bread you bake will get better. So will your patience, your sense of timing, your relationship with the food you make. **The bread is the easy part.** The other things take longer.
