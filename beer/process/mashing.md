# Mashing — for NEIPA and other modern IPAs

The mash sets the **fermentability profile** of your beer — how much of your wort the yeast can eat, how much body remains, how the final beer feels in the mouth. For NEIPAs specifically, the mash also has to **handle a high-adjunct grain bill** (30%+ oats and wheat) without sticking, channeling, or losing efficiency.

This file covers the full mashing phase for IPA brewing: temperature decisions, mash thickness, mash-in technique, the mash-out question, lautering, and the stuck-mash issues that high-adjunct NEIPA grist routinely throws at you.

## What's actually happening in the mash

When malted grain meets hot water in the mash:

1. **Starch gelatinizes** — starch granules absorb water and break open, becoming accessible
2. **α-amylase enzymes** (from the malt) chop starch chains into dextrins (medium chains)
3. **β-amylase enzymes** chop those dextrins into **maltose** (the main fermentable sugar)
4. **Protease enzymes** break some proteins into smaller peptides and amino acids

The **temperature** controls which enzymes are most active:

- **Lower temps (62–65 °C)** favor β-amylase → more maltose → drier, more fermentable beer
- **Higher temps (66–70 °C)** favor α-amylase → more dextrins (longer sugars) → more body, less attenuation
- **Above 72 °C** denatures both enzymes → mash is "done"

This is the **single most important lever** in mashing.

## Mash temperature for NEIPA

NEIPA wants a **medium-bodied beer** — soft, full mouthfeel, but not sweet or cloying. The mash temperature target:

| Goal                           | Mash temp           | Beer character                                |
|--------------------------------|---------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| **NEIPA standard**             | **66–67 °C / 151–153 °F** | Medium body, ~75% attenuation, soft mouthfeel |
| Drier NEIPA / hop creep insurance | 65 °C / 149 °F      | Slightly drier; attenuation 78–80%           |
| Fuller-bodied / hazier        | 68 °C / 154 °F        | More body and dextrins; attenuation 70–73%   |
| WCIPA (drier)                  | 64 °C / 147 °F        | Bone-dry finish; attenuation 80%+             |
| Cold IPA (lager-yeast spec)    | 65 °C / 149 °F        | Lighter body for the dry, crisp profile      |

**For most NEIPA brewers: 66–67 °C is the sweet spot.** It produces a beer with full mouthfeel that doesn't taste sweet.

## Mash thickness

The water-to-grain ratio at mash-in:

| Thickness         | L per kg of grain | Effect                                                |
|-------------------|-------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| Thick mash        | 2.0–2.5 L/kg      | More efficient enzymatic activity; more mash-out water needed |
| **Standard**      | **2.5–3.0 L/kg**  | **The default for most IPAs — balanced**             |
| Thin mash         | 3.0–4.0 L/kg      | Less efficient; faster lauter; more diluted wort    |
| Very thin (BIAB)  | 5.0–7.0 L/kg      | Common in BIAB ("brew in a bag") — full water in mash |

For a typical NEIPA grain bill (~6 kg of grist for a 23 L batch), a **2.7 L/kg ratio** = ~16 L of mash water. Your remaining ~14 L (out of 30 L total brewing water) goes to sparge.

## Mash-in technique

This is where high-adjunct NEIPA grain bills make trouble. Oats and wheat **clump easily** when wet — "dough balls" form, leaving dry flour pockets that don't extract.

### How to mash in cleanly

1. **Heat strike water to ~74 °C / 165 °F** (will drop to mash temp 67 °C when grain hits)
2. **Add salts and acid** to strike water; stir to dissolve
3. **Slowly pour grain into water while stirring continuously** — *not* the other way around
4. **Use a paddle or whisk** to break up any visible clumps as you go
5. **Stir for 30–60 seconds after all grain is in** — make sure no dry pockets remain
6. **Check temp** — should be at mash target (67 °C); if too high, add cold water; if too low, gently heat

### When dough balls happen anyway

- **Lift them out by hand** (with a sanitized tool) and crush them
- **Or stir vigorously** until they break apart
- Some dough balls in oats are inevitable; they'll partially dissolve over the rest

### Avoiding them next time

- **Add grain more slowly** — don't dump the whole bag at once
- **Use a slightly thinner mash** for high-adjunct bills (3.0 L/kg instead of 2.5)
- **Use rice hulls** (see below) — they create channels that prevent compaction
- **Pre-mix the grain** before mash-in — coat dry oats in dry malt to separate them

## Step mashes — when to use them

A "step mash" raises the mash temperature in stages, often holding at lower temps before reaching the main rest. For most NEIPAs, a **single-infusion mash at 67 °C is fine.** But there are cases where steps help:

### β-glucan rest (40–45 °C, 15 min)

For grain bills with **high oat content (>15%)** or **high wheat (>20%)**:

- β-glucanase enzymes break down β-glucans (starchy gum from oats and wheat) at this temp
- Reduces viscosity → faster lauter → less stuck-mash risk
- Some flavor benefit too — less "raw cereal" character

For a 30% oat NEIPA, a 15-min β-glucan rest can dramatically improve lautering.

### Protein rest (50–55 °C, 15 min)

Less needed in modern brewing because malted barley is well-modified, but for **wheat-heavy beers** (Hefeweizens, some Berliner Weisses):

- Protease activity → breaks proteins → less haze, better lauter
- Risk: too much protein breakdown = thin head retention

For NEIPA, **skip this rest** — you want the proteins for haze and body.

### Saccharification rest (65–67 °C, 60 min)

The main mash rest. Where the bulk of the conversion happens.

### Mash-out (75–78 °C, 10 min)

Discussed in detail below.

### When to do step mashing

Step mashes need either:

- A **HERMS** or **RIMS** system (continuously recirculating wort through a heat exchanger), OR
- A **direct-fired mash tun** that can be heated mid-mash

For BIAB or basic 3-vessel setups, **single-infusion is the practical default.** A protein-rich, well-stirred mash with rice hulls accomplishes most of what a β-glucan rest would, without equipment.

## Mash-out — what it is and whether to do it

"Mash-out" = **raising the mash temperature to ~75–78 °C / 168–172 °F** at the end of the conversion phase.

What it does:

- **Denatures the enzymes** → "locks in" the wort sugar profile
- **Reduces wort viscosity** → faster, easier lauter
- **Stops further conversion** → no more attenuation changes from prolonged contact

What it doesn't do:

- **Doesn't significantly improve efficiency** in most home brewing setups
- **Doesn't solve stuck-mash issues** caused by high-adjunct gummy grist

### When to mash-out

**Do it for:**

- **Long lauters** (10+ minutes) where prolonged enzyme activity could shift the wort profile
- **High-adjunct grain bills** (oats, wheat) — the temperature reduces wort viscosity for cleaner lautering
- **Step mashes** — the natural endpoint of a controlled-temperature mash

**Skip it for:**

- **Quick lauters** in BIAB (just lift the bag, no extended drainage)
- **Basic recipes** without high adjunct content
- **Time-constrained brew days** where the 10 minutes matters

### How to mash-out

If your system supports it (HERMS / RIMS / direct-fired):

1. **At end of saccharification rest**, gradually raise mash temp to **76 °C / 169 °F**
2. **Hold for 10 minutes**
3. **Begin lauter / sparge**

For BIAB or basic cooler-mash setups: **mash-out by sparging with very hot water (78 °C)** instead of stepping the mash itself. Same effect, different mechanism.

## Lautering / sparging

The "lauter" or "sparge" phase = **draining the wort from the mash and rinsing the grain bed**.

### BIAB / single-vessel approach

- **Lift the grain bag** when mash is done
- **Let it drain** into the kettle (don't squeeze hard — releases tannins)
- **No separate sparge** typically — full water is in the mash from the start
- **Optionally**: a "mini-sparge" with 2–4 L of 76 °C water poured over the bag for a small efficiency boost

### 3-vessel approach

- **Vorlauf**: recirculate the first cloudy wort back through the grain bed for 5–10 minutes until clear
- **Drain main wort** into the kettle slowly (15–30 minutes)
- **Sparge with hot water (76 °C)** to rinse remaining sugars off the grain
- **Continue draining** until pre-boil volume is collected

### "Fly sparging" vs "batch sparging"

- **Fly sparge** — slow continuous water addition matched to drainage rate. Higher efficiency, takes 45–60 min.
- **Batch sparge** — drain all main wort, then add sparge water all at once, drain again. Faster (15–20 min), slightly lower efficiency.

For most home brewers: **batch sparging is the practical choice**. Fly sparging is for when you have time and want every drop of efficiency.

## Stuck mash — diagnosis and fix

A "stuck mash" = wort doesn't drain (or drains very slowly) from the grain bed.

### Why high-adjunct NEIPA grain bills stick

- **Oats** contain β-glucans — sticky polysaccharides that form a gel
- **Wheat** has more proteins than barley — sticky when wet
- **Together with fine-crushed malt**, the bed compacts into an impermeable layer

### Prevention (the easy fix)

**Rice hulls.** Add **5–8% of grain bill weight in rice hulls** (~250–500 g for a typical NEIPA batch). They:

- Create **physical channels** in the grain bed
- Don't add flavor or fermentables (they're inert)
- Cost almost nothing
- Are the **single most useful trick for high-adjunct mashing**

For any NEIPA with >20% oats + wheat, **always use rice hulls**.

### Mid-mash recovery

If the mash is stuck despite rice hulls:

1. **Stop draining**
2. **Recirculate** (BrewZilla / RIMS pumps) — even 5 min of pump time loosens the bed
3. **Stir the top of the grain bed gently** (don't disturb the false bottom layer)
4. **Add 2 L of hot sparge water** to break up compaction
5. **Try again** with slower drain rate

### Last-resort recovery

If still stuck after 30+ minutes:

1. **Lift the grain bag** (BIAB) or break up the bed (3-vessel)
2. **Drain everything you can**
3. **Squeeze gently** — yes, you'll extract some tannins, but you've lost the batch otherwise
4. **Boil and continue** — accept slightly lower efficiency
5. **Next time:** more rice hulls, thinner mash, slower drain

## Vorlauf — clearing the wort

Before the main lauter, "vorlauf" = recirculating the first cloudy wort back through the grain bed for clarity.

For 3-vessel:

1. **Open the lauter valve** slightly — collect 2 L of cloudy wort
2. **Pour gently back over the top of the grain bed**
3. **Repeat for 5–10 minutes** until the wort runs clear

This step **filters out grain particles and small bits of dough ball** before they end up in your kettle. **Skip for BIAB** — the bag itself does the filtering.

## NEIPA-specific mash strategy

Putting it all together for a typical NEIPA grain bill (65% pilsner, 15% oats, 15% wheat, 5% carapils):

### Pre-mash

- **Strike water 74 °C, salts + acid added**
- **Rice hulls measured** (5% of grain bill = ~300 g)

### Mash-in

- **Slow grain addition with continuous stirring**
- **Add rice hulls between layers** of grain (so they distribute through the bed)
- **Final mash temp: 67 °C**

### Saccharification rest

- **60 minutes at 67 °C**
- **No need to step mash** for typical NEIPA bills

### Mash-out

- **Optional but helpful** for high-adjunct: 10 min at 76 °C
- BIAB equivalent: sparge water at 78 °C

### Lauter

- **Vorlauf 5 min** (3-vessel)
- **Slow drain** (don't compact the bed)
- **Batch sparge** for typical setups
- **Target pre-boil volume:** ~28 L for a 23 L into-fermenter batch

### What success looks like

- **Clear wort** by the end of vorlauf
- **Steady lauter rate** (not stalling)
- **No stuck mash**
- **Mash efficiency: 70–80%** for most home setups

## Mash differences across IPA family

| Style       | Mash temp     | Step mash?    | Mash thickness  | Adjuncts                              |
|-------------|---------------|---------------|------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **NEIPA**   | 66–67 °C      | Optional β-glucan if oats >20% | 2.7 L/kg | 30% (oats + wheat)                  |
| **WCIPA**   | 64 °C         | No            | 2.5 L/kg         | None (95% pale + 5% crystal)          |
| **Cold IPA**| 65 °C         | No            | 2.7 L/kg         | 20% rice or corn (ferments out)       |
| **DIPA**    | 64–65 °C (low for fermentability) | No | 2.5 L/kg | 5–10% sugar (added in boil, not mash) |

The same brewer can pull all four off with the same kit — just different mash temps and grain bills.

## What separates great mashes from OK mashes

1. **Hitting target temperature on the first try** — preheat strike water carefully; account for the 5–7 °C drop on grain addition
2. **No dough balls** — slow grain addition + continuous stirring
3. **No stuck mash** — rice hulls are cheap insurance
4. **Mash pH in target range** (5.3–5.4) — water + acid management
5. **Clean lauter** (no excessive grain in the kettle) — vorlauf
6. **Documented in your log** — temp at each stage, time, observations

Master this once and it transfers across all your brewing.
