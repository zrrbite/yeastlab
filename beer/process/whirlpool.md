# Whirlpool & hopstand

The whirlpool / hopstand is **the highest-leverage flavor step in NEIPA brewing**. It's where most of your aroma load goes in, and the temperature you choose determines how much bitterness you accidentally pick up along the way.

## What the whirlpool actually does

Traditionally, "whirlpool" means **swirling hot wort to create a debris cone in the center of the kettle** — collecting trub (hot break, hop matter, protein flocs) for easier separation. Modern usage extends "whirlpool" to mean **post-boil hop additions**: hops added after flameout, allowed to steep at controlled temperature, then chilled.

For NEIPA, the whirlpool serves three functions:

1. **Aroma extraction** at temperatures **below boiling** — preserves volatile oils that would have boiled off during a kettle addition.
2. **Limited isomerization** — at the right temperature, alpha acids isomerize slowly. Some bittering happens but you have control.
3. **Trub formation** — proteins and polyphenols still coagulate at warm temps, leaving cleaner wort.

## The temperature window

This is the central decision of a whirlpool addition.

| Temp                | What happens                                                              |
|---------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 100 °C (212 °F) — boiling | Full isomerization, full aroma stripping. This is just a 0-min hop addition. |
| 90 °C (194 °F)      | Aggressive isomerization, significant aroma loss. Some "old-school" recipes call for this. |
| **82 °C (180 °F)**  | **Janish's recommendation.** Real isomerization (~10 IBU per 30 min for typical alpha hops) but good oil retention. |
| 77 °C (170 °F)      | Common older NEIPA recommendation. Less isomerization, very good oil retention. |
| 71 °C (160 °F)      | Minimal isomerization. Mostly aroma extraction. Some find this temp too low — reduces some oil extraction too. |
| < 65 °C (149 °F)    | No isomerization but also reduced oil extraction. Borderline "cold steep."  |

Janish (2021) recommends **180 °F (82 °C)** for the whirlpool. The argument:

- Reasonable IBU contribution (helps offset iso-alpha-acid adsorption from heavy dry hopping — see [`../hops/dry-hopping.md`](../hops/dry-hopping.md))
- Better extraction of survivable hop compounds (esters, monoterpene alcohols, thiols) than colder whirlpools
- Still well below boiling, so myrcene and other very volatile compounds are largely preserved

The **older "NEIPA orthodoxy" of 170 °F (77 °C)** is also fine, especially if you're going very heavy on dry hopping and want to minimize all whirlpool-derived bitterness. But Janish's data (and Sapwood's results) support 180 °F as the sweet spot.

## Survivable-compound hops for the whirlpool

Janish (2021) recommends these hops specifically for the whirlpool because their characteristic compounds **survive the heat well**:

- **Idaho 7** — high in 2MIB ester
- **Mosaic** — diverse compound profile, holds up
- **Bravo** — high in fatty acids that enhance fruity perception
- **Citra** — broad survivable profile
- **Millennium** — cleaner, can serve as supporting hop
- **Mount Hood** — solid noble-leaning option
- **Ekuanot** — high in 2MIB and isoamyl isobutyrate esters
- **Simcoe** — solid all-rounder

This doesn't mean *only* these hops in the whirlpool. But these are particularly **rewarded** by hot-side aroma additions because their key compounds aren't volatile losses.

## Whirlpool dosing

For a NEIPA, Janish recommends matching your dry hop rate with **1.5–2.5 lb/bbl** of whirlpool hops at 180 °F. In metric:

| Style intensity      | Whirlpool rate | Notes                                  |
|----------------------|----------------|----------------------------------------|
| Soft hazy            | 4–5 g/L        | ~110 g for 23 L batch                  |
| Standard NEIPA       | 5–8 g/L        | ~120–185 g for 23 L batch              |
| Aggressive NEIPA     | 8–10 g/L       | ~185–230 g for 23 L batch              |
| DIPA                 | 10–12 g/L      | Compounds polyphenol + pH issues       |

These are big additions. A standard West Coast IPA might use 30–60 g of whirlpool hops; a NEIPA can use 200 g+.

## The procedure

For a typical homebrew batch with an immersion chiller:

1. **Boil wort 60 min** with bittering hops at 60 min.
2. **Flameout.** Stop the boil.
3. **Cool to whirlpool temp.** Insert immersion chiller, run cold water until you hit **82 °C / 180 °F**. Should take 5–8 minutes.
4. **Stop chilling. Stir wort to create whirlpool motion.**
5. **Add whirlpool hops.** Stir gently to incorporate.
6. **Maintain temperature.** Cover the kettle. Optionally insulate it.
7. **Stand 20–30 minutes.** Most extraction happens in the first 15 minutes; longer adds incremental polyphenol.
8. **Resume chilling.** Bring to fermentation temp (typically 18–20 °C).
9. **Transfer to fermenter.** Try to leave the trub/hop debris behind in the kettle — this is what the whirlpool cone is for.

### Single vs split whirlpool additions

Some brewers split the whirlpool into two additions at different temperatures:

- **First addition at 90–95 °C** (right after flameout) for the survivable-compound hops you want isomerized to medium IBU.
- **Second addition at 75–80 °C** (after partial chilling) for the very volatile aromatic hops you want to preserve fully.

Diminishing returns at homebrew scale. **A single 82 °C addition with a 25-minute stand is plenty for almost any NEIPA.**

## Common mistakes

- **Whirlpool too hot.** If you add hops at 95 °C+ you've just done a 0-min boil addition, not a true whirlpool. Aroma loss is significant.
- **Whirlpool too long.** 30+ minutes pulls extra polyphenols without extra aroma. Keep contact 20–30 min max.
- **Whirlpool with bittering-only hops.** CTZ, Magnum, Warrior in a whirlpool is mostly a waste — they don't have the aromatic punch to justify the position. Use them at the 60-min boil addition instead.
- **Skipping whirlpool entirely.** If you put all your hops in dry hopping, you miss the survivable hot-side compounds (esters, much of the monoterpene alcohol fraction). Whirlpool isn't optional for a good NEIPA.
- **Inadequate chilling between flameout and whirlpool addition.** If you can't drop the kettle from 100 °C to 82 °C in under 10 minutes, your "whirlpool" is happening at 90 °C+ and it's effectively a hot kettle addition. Use a counter-flow chiller, plate chiller, or cold water immersion to drop temp quickly.

## After the whirlpool

Chill the wort to fermentation temperature (typically **18–20 °C** for NEIPA) **as fast as you can**. Slow chilling between 80 °C and 30 °C is when:

- Wort is most vulnerable to oxidation
- DMS can form (especially with pilsner-base recipes)
- Bacteria can grow (the danger zone)

For homebrew scale: a good immersion chiller + cold tap water + agitation should drop a 23 L batch from 80 °C to 20 °C in 15–20 minutes. If your tap water is warm, pre-chill with ice or use a counterflow chiller.

## Whirlpool vs hop stand — terminology

The terms get used interchangeably but mean slightly different things:

- **Whirlpool** — the *process* of swirling hot wort to create a debris cone (separates trub from wort). Originally a separation step, not a hop addition step.
- **Hop stand** — a *steady soak* of hops at a controlled post-boil temperature. May or may not involve swirling.
- **Modern usage** — "whirlpool addition" usually means hops added during a hop stand at controlled temperature, with or without actual whirlpool motion.

For homebrew scale, the distinction is academic. **You're adding hops to hot post-boil wort and letting them steep for 20–30 minutes at a target temperature.** Whether you're swirling or not matters less than the temperature you hit.

## Practical mechanics — creating a vortex

Real whirlpool action (the swirling that pulls trub to the center) needs **physical agitation**. Options:

### Manual whirlpool

The simplest:

- **Insert a long sanitized paddle** into the kettle
- **Stir vigorously in one direction** for 30–60 seconds — get the wort spinning
- **Let it stand 25 minutes** — the spinning continues, slowly creating the debris cone in the center
- **The trub forms a cone** that you can drain around when transferring

### Pump-driven whirlpool

If you have a pump (BrewZilla, etc.):

- **Connect output to a tangential whirlpool port** in the kettle wall
- **Pump for 5 minutes** to start the spin
- **Let stand 25 minutes**
- The pump can be off during the stand

### "No-whirlpool" hop stand

You can skip the actual swirling entirely:

- **Add hops to wort, cover kettle, let stand** at controlled temperature for 25–30 minutes
- **No vortex, but the hop oils still extract**
- **Trub doesn't separate as cleanly** — you get more solids in the fermenter
- **Practical reality for many homebrewers** — works fine, just slightly more cleanup

For NEIPA specifically: the **trub doesn't matter much** because hop solids in the fermenter don't meaningfully hurt NEIPA quality (and NEIPA fermentation drops a lot of solids anyway). For WCIPA where you want clarity, **proper whirlpool with vortex helps**.

## Multi-temperature whirlpool strategies

A single 82 °C addition is the standard. **Splitting whirlpool additions across temperatures** is an advanced move that some brewers swear by:

### Split at flameout / cooled

- **First addition at flameout (98 °C)** for the survivable-compound hops where you want some isomerization
- **Second addition at 75 °C** for very volatile aromatic hops you want to fully preserve

### Three-stage hot-side

- **80% of bittering at 60 min** in boil
- **Aroma hops 1 at flameout** — partial isomerization, full extraction
- **Aroma hops 2 at 80 °C whirlpool** — minimal isomerization
- **Aroma hops 3 at 65 °C whirlpool** — pure aroma, almost like cold steep

The diminishing returns kick in fast at homebrew scale. **A single 82 °C whirlpool stand is excellent for almost any IPA.** Multi-temperature is for brewers chasing the last 5%.

## Cold steep / "dry hop in the kettle"

A modern technique: adding hops to wort **after it's already been chilled** (below 60 °C), letting it steep cool for an hour or more.

### What it does

- **Almost zero isomerization** — pure aroma extraction
- **Different compounds extract** than at 80 °C — closer to dry-hop character
- **More polyphenol extraction** than typical whirlpool (wort acidity at warm temps)

### When to use

- **NEIPA** — adds hop character close to "dry hop in the kettle" without the dry-hop window
- **High-aroma styles** where you want maximum extraction without heat
- **Beers where biotransformation isn't planned** (no active-ferm dry hop) — the cold steep partially compensates

### Procedure

1. Whirlpool as normal at 82 °C (your main aroma load)
2. **Continue chilling to ~50–60 °C / 122–140 °F**
3. **Add a "cold steep" hop charge** — typically 2–3 g/L
4. **Let stand 30–60 minutes** at this temperature
5. **Resume chilling to pitch temp** and transfer

This adds significant complexity to the brew day. **Most NEIPA brewers don't do this** — sticking with whirlpool + biotransformation dry hop + cold-side dry hop is enough.

## The "double whirlpool" concept

A small number of pro brewers (Treehouse-influenced) do **two separate whirlpool steps**:

- **First whirlpool at 82 °C, 25 min** — main aroma load
- **Drain wort to a second clean vessel**, leaving trub behind
- **Add a second whirlpool charge to the now-clean wort** at 82 °C, another 20 min
- Then chill and transfer

The argument: avoids extracting tannins and polyphenols from the first round of trub. The reality at homebrew scale: **probably overkill**, and adds significant complexity to the brew day.

If you have the equipment for it (two kettles), worth experimenting. For most brewers, single whirlpool wins on time/effort vs marginal quality gain.

## Acidification timing during whirlpool

Per Janish, acidifying wort to **post-boil pH 4.8–4.9** improves NEIPA character (lower bitterness perception, better drinkability, better hop expression). Timing matters:

### Where to add post-boil acid

- **At end of boil (last 5 min)** — phosphoric or lactic acid into the kettle. Lets the boil mix the acid evenly.
- **At start of whirlpool** — acid added with whirlpool hops. Same effect but the hops are also doing work.
- **After whirlpool, before chilling** — works but you have less time for the acid to mix.

For practical brewing: **add acid at the last 5 minutes of boil**. By the time you start the whirlpool, the wort pH is in the right range.

### How much

- **Typical:** 1–3 mL of 10% phosphoric acid for 28–30 L of pre-boil wort
- **Test with pH meter** — should read ~4.8–4.9
- **Adjust for next batch** based on the result

## Hop creep risk during whirlpool

Hop creep usually happens during **active fermentation dry hopping** when hop enzymes ferment residual dextrins. But it can also start in the whirlpool if:

- **Whirlpool is too hot** for too long, denaturing wort enzymes that would otherwise compete
- **Wort sits at warm temps** before yeast pitch, allowing hop diastatic enzymes to work

Mitigation:

- **Don't extend whirlpool past 30 minutes**
- **Chill quickly** to fermentation temp after whirlpool
- **Pitch yeast promptly** — the longer wort sits at 18 °C without yeast, the more potential for unwanted enzymatic activity

## When the whirlpool goes wrong

### "My beer tastes vegetal / soapy"

Possible whirlpool causes:

- **Whirlpool too long** (>40 min) at warm temp — over-extracted polyphenols
- **Hops too cool during whirlpool** (<60 °C) — green / grassy notes
- **Old hops** — degraded compounds amplified by heat

### "My beer is more bitter than expected"

- **Whirlpool too hot** (>90 °C) — more isomerization than calculated
- **Long whirlpool stand** at moderate temp — slow continuous isomerization

### "My beer lacks hop aroma despite heavy whirlpool"

- **Whirlpool too late in chill** — wort already too cool, less extraction
- **Hops not in good contact with wort** — bag, basket, or pile didn't mix
- **Old hops** — character was already gone before brewing

## Whirlpool checklist

Before starting whirlpool:

- [ ] **Boil completed** (60–90 min depending on style)
- [ ] **Bittering charge already added** (60 min mark)
- [ ] **Acid added in last 5 min of boil** (post-boil pH ~4.8–4.9)
- [ ] **Hop additions weighed and labeled** for whirlpool
- [ ] **Chilling water hookup ready** (immersion chiller installed)

During whirlpool:

- [ ] **Temperature reached 82 °C** before adding hops
- [ ] **Hops added and stirred** to incorporate
- [ ] **Kettle covered** to maintain temperature
- [ ] **Optional: vortex started** with paddle or pump
- [ ] **Timer set 25 min**

After whirlpool:

- [ ] **Resume chilling promptly** (don't extend whirlpool by accident)
- [ ] **Cool to pitch temp 18–20 °C**
- [ ] **Transfer wort to fermenter** (try to leave trub in kettle)
- [ ] **Pitch yeast immediately** after oxygenation
