# Mixed-fermentation & sour beers

The wildest territory in beer. **Beers fermented with multiple microbes** — typically *Saccharomyces* yeast + *Brettanomyces* + lactic acid bacteria + sometimes acetic acid bacteria. The result is **complex, often sour, often funky beers** that can age for years and develop further complexity over time.

This file is a compact overview of a vast category. Mixed-fermentation is its own multi-year discipline; this just maps the territory.

## The mixed-fermentation universe

| Style                       | Origin           | Defining feature                                       |
|-----------------------------|------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| **Lambic** (Gueuze)         | Belgium          | Spontaneous fermentation; aged 1–3 years; champagne-like |
| **Flemish Red**             | Belgium          | Brett + lactic; oak-aged; balsamic-like               |
| **Berliner Weisse**         | Germany          | Quick lactic souring; 3% ABV; sour wheat beer          |
| **Gose**                    | Germany          | Lactic + salt + coriander; sour and salty             |
| **Brett pale ale**          | Modern American  | 100% Brett or Sacc + Brett; not sour, just funky      |
| **Brett saison**            | Modern Belgian/American | Saison + Brett; dry, funky, complex            |
| **Sour IPA / kettle sour**  | Modern American  | Quick lactic acid + IPA hop bill                      |
| **Wild ales**               | Various          | "Sour but no specific tradition" — a catch-all        |

## The microbes

### *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*

Standard ale yeast. **Primary fermentation does most of the alcohol production.**

### *Brettanomyces*

A separate genus of yeast — slow, oxygen-tolerant, produces **distinctive funky character**:

- **Barnyard, hay, leather, "horse blanket"** — at high concentrations
- **Tropical fruit, pineapple, mango** — at low/moderate concentrations
- **4-ethylphenol (4EP)** — phenolic, smoky character
- **Acetic acid** when oxygen present — vinegar character

Brett strains:

- **Brettanomyces bruxellensis** — most common; aggressive funk
- **Brettanomyces claussenii** — milder, more fruit-forward
- **Brettanomyces lambicus** — tart, tropical

### Lactic acid bacteria (LAB)

Same family as in sourdough:

- **Lactobacillus** — produces lactic acid; clean tartness
- **Pediococcus** — slower; produces lactic + sometimes diacetyl ("buttery sour")

### Acetic acid bacteria (AAB)

Vinegar-producing bacteria — *Acetobacter*. **Requires oxygen.** In barrel-aged sours, controlled oxygen exposure produces **acetic character** (think balsamic vinegar character in a Flemish Red).

## How styles combine these

- **Lambic:** spontaneous (whatever wild microbes are in the air during cooling) + extended barrel aging
- **Flemish Red:** Sacc + LAB + Brett + AAB (controlled O₂ exposure in barrels)
- **Berliner Weisse:** Sacc + Lacto only (no Brett); fast turnaround
- **Gose:** Sacc + Lacto + salt + coriander
- **Brett saison:** Sacc + Brett aged 3–6 months
- **Modern wild ale:** any combination, brewer's choice

## Vital stats — typical sour ale

| Parameter | Range |
|-----------|-------|
| OG | 1.044–1.055 (lower than NEIPAs; sour is lighter) |
| FG | 1.000–1.005 (very dry; LAB and Brett finish wort) |
| ABV | 4.5–6.0% |
| pH | **3.2–3.6** (vs IPA's 4.2–4.4; perceptibly sour) |
| IBU | 5–15 (very low; bitterness clashes with sour) |
| Carbonation | 3.0+ vol (champagne-like) |

## Recipe template — quick kettle sour

For brewers who want sour without years of aging — kettle souring uses LAB **only in the kettle**, then boils the soured wort to kill the bacteria, then ferments with regular yeast.

For 23 L of "easy sour ale" at OG 1.045:

| Ingredient                    | Amount   |
|-------------------------------|----------|
| Pilsner malt                  | 3500 g   |
| Wheat malt                    | 700 g    |
| Acidulated malt               | 100 g    |
| Hops — Hallertau 60 min       | 8 g      |
| Yeast: Wyeast 1056 American Ale | 11 g dry |
| **Lactobacillus plantarum** (Goodbelly probiotic shot OR Wyeast 5335) | 100 g of starter |

### Process

1. **Mash** at 65 °C × 60 min.
2. **No boil yet.** Cool wort to **40 °C / 104 °F** in the kettle.
3. **Pitch Lacto culture** — Goodbelly shots (consumer probiotic) work great. Add ~100 g of liquid Lacto culture.
4. **Hold at 38–40 °C for 24–48 hours**, covered. Use heat wraps or a brewbelt to maintain temp.
5. **Check pH every 12 hours.** Target pH **3.4** — usually reached in 24–36 h.
6. **When sour enough, BOIL for 60 minutes** — this kills the Lacto, isomerizes the small hop addition, sterilizes the wort.
7. **Cool, transfer to fermenter, oxygenate, pitch ale yeast.**
8. **Ferment 7–10 days** at 18–20 °C.
9. **Optional fruit additions** in secondary (raspberry, peach, etc.).
10. **Carbonate high** (3.0+ vol).

Total time: **3–4 weeks** brew-to-keg. Much faster than traditional sour beers.

## Recipe template — Brett saison

For 23 L of dry, funky, complex saison at OG 1.060:

| Ingredient                    | Amount   |
|-------------------------------|----------|
| Belgian pilsner malt          | 4000 g   |
| Wheat malt                    | 800 g    |
| Munich malt                   | 200 g    |
| Cane sugar (10 min addition)  | 400 g    |
| Hops — Saaz 60 min            | 25 g     |
| Hops — Saaz 5 min             | 15 g     |
| Yeast — Wyeast 3711 French Saison (primary) | 1 starter |
| **Brettanomyces bruxellensis** (secondary) | 1 vial Wyeast 5112 / WLP650 |

### Process

1. **Brew and ferment** as a normal saison.
2. **After 2 weeks of saison fermentation**, pitch Brett into the secondary.
3. **Age 3–6 months** at 18–22 °C, sealed.
4. **Develops barnyard, leather, dry-fruit character.**
5. **Bottle condition** with extra yeast (Brett activity is unpredictable; bottle bombs are a risk if you carbonate too much).

## Equipment considerations

Mixed-fermentation has **separate equipment requirements**:

### Once-touched dedicated equipment

- **Brett can colonize plastic and porous surfaces** (silicone, Tygon hose, plastic fermenters, chest freezer linings)
- Once a vessel has had Brett or LAB in it, **dedicate it permanently** to sour/wild brewing
- A common mistake: brew a Brett beer in your normal NEIPA fermenter → next NEIPA tastes funky / Brett-infected

For a homebrewer who wants to do mixed-ferm:

- **Separate fermenter** (cheap plastic bucket is fine — you'll dedicate it)
- **Separate hoses, racking canes, airlocks**
- **Separate kegs / packaging equipment** ideally; or use bottles for wild/sour beers
- **Stainless steel survives cleaning** if you sanitize carefully — a stainless conical can be used for both clean and wild if cleaned thoroughly between

### Glass / stainless are safer than plastic

If you only have one fermenter, stainless is more forgiving than plastic. PET plastic is highly susceptible to Brett colonization.

## Aging mixed-fermentation beers

- **Patience** is the defining requirement
- **Sour beers improve over months and years** in a way clean beers don't
- **Bottle conditioning** is traditional — sediment in the bottle is fine
- **Store cool but not refrigerated** for aging (~12–18 °C)
- **Don't refrigerate until you're ready to drink** — refrigeration slows but doesn't stop development

## What "good sour" tastes like

- **Tart, not aggressive** — pleasant acidic edge, not eye-watering
- **Complex, not muddy** — distinct flavor notes (fruit, funk, oak, etc.)
- **Drinkable** — saliva-stimulating, refreshing
- **Layered finish** — flavor develops as you sip and as the beer warms
- **Aroma matches taste** — fragrant, not just sour-smelling

A bad sour:

- **One-note acidic** — just sour with no character
- **Vinegar-dominant** — too much acetic, harsh
- **Off-funk** — band-aid, plastic, sweat (wrong microbe took over)
- **Fizzy water with lemon** — under-fermented, hollow

## Why mixed-fermentation

Reasons to brew mixed-ferm beers:

- **Complexity.** No clean beer can match the layered character.
- **Aging.** Beers that improve over years.
- **Food pairing.** Sour ales are extraordinary with food.
- **Tradition.** Some of the oldest beer-brewing traditions (Lambic).
- **Experimentation.** Each batch develops differently; surprise is part of the appeal.

Reasons not to:

- **Patience required.** Months to years.
- **Equipment dedication.** Permanent commitment.
- **Variability.** Can taste very different from your intent.
- **Acquired taste.** Not for everyone; clean-beer drinkers may not appreciate.
