# Pilsner

The most-consumed beer style in the world (in volume) and **the hardest beer to brew well at home**. A pale, crisp, bitter lager with **no place to hide flaws** — every off-flavor stands out, every shortcut is detectable. Brewers who can make a great pilsner can usually make anything.

Pilsner originated in 1842 in Plzeň, Czechia, when a Bavarian brewer (Josef Groll) was hired to fix a city's bad beer. The result — golden, clear, bitter — was unprecedented and became the template for nearly all modern beer.

## The two main pilsners

The style splits into two distinct traditions:

### Czech Pilsner (Pilsner Urquell tradition)

- **More malt-forward** — pronounced bready / cracker pilsner malt character
- **Heavily Saaz-hopped** — 35–45 IBU of noble Saaz character
- **Decoction-mashed traditionally** — produces bready depth that infusion mashing doesn't
- **Soft Bohemian water** — low minerals, perfect for soft expression
- **Original gravity 1.044–1.052**, modest ABV

### German Pilsner (Northern German tradition)

- **More bitter** — 30–40 IBU but feels sharper due to harder water
- **Cleaner, drier** — less malt sweetness than Czech
- **Higher attenuation** — finishes drier
- **Hallertau, Tettnang, Spalt hops** — German noble varieties
- **OG 1.044–1.050**

The American "macro lager" (Bud, etc.) is descended from German Pilsner via 19th-century German immigration, but with corn/rice adjuncts and dramatically less hop. Different beast.

## Grain bill — pilsner malt only

Both styles use almost entirely **pilsner malt** — a low-color, fully modified European-style 2-row malt. The differences:

- **Czech:** Czech pilsner malt (Bohemian Floor Malted preferred) at 100%
- **German:** German pilsner malt at 95–100%, sometimes 5% Munich for body

**No other malt is needed.** Crystal, caramel, Munich (in significant amounts), or roast malts move the beer out of style.

## Water

Each style demands different water:

### Czech Pilsner water (very soft)

- Ca: 5–10 ppm
- Mg: trace
- SO₄: trace
- Cl: trace
- HCO₃: 5–15 ppm
- Total dissolved solids: <50 ppm

This is essentially **distilled water with a tiny amount of mineral**. The famously soft Bohemian water of Plzeň allows the malt and hops to express delicately without mineral interference.

### German Pilsner water (still soft, slightly more mineralized)

- Ca: 50–80 ppm
- Mg: 5–10 ppm
- SO₄: 50–80 ppm (more sulfate for hop sharpness)
- Cl: 30–50 ppm
- HCO₃: 30–50 ppm
- TDS: 100–150 ppm

Slightly harder than Czech, slightly more sulfate to push the hop bitterness. Pretty similar to a Cold IPA water profile.

## Hops

### Czech Pilsner

- **100% Saaz** is traditional. Earthy, slightly spicy, classic noble hop character.
- Schedule: 60 min, 30 min, 15 min, 5 min — layered Saaz across the boil

### German Pilsner

- **Hallertau Mittelfrüh, Tettnang, Spalt** — German noble hops
- **Sometimes more aggressive bittering** with magnum at 60 min for clean IBU, then noble hops late

## Yeast

Lager yeast — same strains as Cold IPA but with **traditional cold fermentation**:

- **Wyeast 2001 Pilsner Urquell H Strain / WLP800 Pilsner Lager** — Czech Pilsner yeast; lightly fruity
- **Wyeast 2007 Pilsen Lager / WLP838 Southern German Lager** — German Pilsner yeast; clean, crisp
- **Saflager W-34/70 (dry)** — universal lager yeast; works for both styles
- **Saflager W-31/70** — slightly drier, very similar to W-34/70

## Process — slow lager fermentation

Pilsner is a **patient brewer's beer**. Fast pilsner = bad pilsner.

### 1. Mash

- **Czech:** decoction mash if possible (pull a portion of the mash, boil it, return). Adds bready depth. Single-infusion at 65 °C also works; you lose some character.
- **German:** single-infusion at 65 °C is fine

### 2. Boil

- **90 min boil** (longer than IPAs to drive off DMS — pilsner malt produces more DMS than other malts)
- Add hops as scheduled

### 3. Pitch big

- Lager fermentation needs **2–3× the cell count** of equivalent ale
- Make a **2.5 L starter** from one liquid yeast pack, OR use **22 g (2 packs) of dry W-34/70** for 23 L

### 4. Ferment cold and slow

- **Pitch at 9–10 °C / 48–50 °F** into well-oxygenated wort (lager yeast needs O₂)
- **Hold at 10–12 °C / 50–54 °F** for **2–3 weeks** until FG approached
- **Diacetyl rest:** raise to 18 °C for 2–3 days. **Critical** — lager yeast diacetyl needs warm cleanup.
- **Cold crash to 0–2 °C** for **4–8 weeks** of lagering

### 5. Lager (the long sleep)

This is the part that separates real pilsner from "lager-yeast IPA" — **sustained cold storage at 0–2 °C for at least 4 weeks, ideally 6–8.**

What's happening during lagering:

- Yeast and proteins drop out → brilliantly clear beer
- Sulfur compounds dissipate → cleaner cup
- Flavors integrate
- Slight residual fermentation cleans up sugars

Skipping or shortening this step is the #1 reason home-brewed pilsners are mediocre. **Be patient.**

### 6. Carbonate

- 2.5 vol Czech, 2.7 vol German
- Either spunding (best — natural carbonation) or force-carbonate after lagering

## Total timeline

- Brew day: day 0
- Active fermentation: days 0–14
- Diacetyl rest: days 14–17
- Lagering: days 17–60+
- **Drink: day 60–80**

A pilsner from grain to glass is **8–10 weeks**. There are no shortcuts.

## What "good pilsner" tastes like

### Czech

- **Pronounced pilsner malt** — bready, slightly sweet, doughy character
- **Saaz hop character** — earthy, slightly spicy, classic noble
- **Balanced bitterness** — present but harmonious
- **Soft mouthfeel** with full body for the ABV
- **Clean fermentation** — no yeast character

### German

- **Crisp, dry, biting bitterness**
- **Noble hop character** — herbal, slightly grassy
- **Very dry finish**
- **Lean body**
- **Sulfur subtle** (slight character is OK in German pilsner; should not be dominant)

Both should be **brilliantly clear, golden, with persistent white head**.

## Why pilsner is hard

- **No place to hide.** Off-flavors are obvious.
- **Long timeline** — patience required
- **Big yeast pitches** — need a stir plate or multiple packs
- **Cold control** — need a fridge / chamber
- **DMS risk** — long boil, vigorous chill, attention to mash temp
- **Diacetyl risk** — must do the rest properly
- **Water** — needs RO + remineralization; tap won't do
- **Decoction** (Czech) — extra step, takes hours

A great pilsner is a **brewer's quiet flex**. Most home brewers don't bother.

## Common pilsner mistakes

- **Pitching too small.** Underpitched lager → off-flavors. Pitch big.
- **Pitching too warm.** Lager yeast at 22 °C produces banana esters. Pitch at 10 °C and ferment cold.
- **Skipping diacetyl rest.** Result: slick, butter-flavored beer that won't fade.
- **Skipping lagering.** Beer is hazy, sulfury, immature.
- **Boiling too short.** DMS character — corn or cooked-vegetable off-flavor.
- **Wrong water.** Pilsner is the most water-sensitive style. Soft, low-bicarbonate water for Czech; slightly harder for German.

## Recipe template — German Pilsner

For 23 L at OG 1.048:

| Ingredient                | Amount  |
|---------------------------|---------|
| German pilsner malt       | 4500 g  |
| Optional Munich           | 200 g   |
| Hops:                     |         |
| - Magnum 60 min           | 18 g (~30 IBU) |
| - Hallertau 15 min        | 25 g (~5 IBU)  |
| - Hallertau 5 min         | 25 g (~3 IBU)  |
| Yeast: W-34/70            | 22 g (2 packs) |

**Mash:** 65 °C, 60 min
**Boil:** 90 min
**Ferment:** 11 °C × 14 days, 18 °C × 3 days (D-rest), then 0 °C × 6 weeks lager
**Carb:** 2.7 vol

Drink at week 8.
