# House recipes — building a rotation

The fastest path from "intermediate brewer" to "consistently great brewer" is **brewing the same recipes repeatedly with deliberate iteration**. This file is a framework for building a rotation of 3–5 "house" recipes you brew often enough to truly master.

The opposite approach — brewing a different recipe every time — produces a lot of variety but never produces mastery of any single beer.

## Why have a house rotation

### Pattern recognition compounds

By brew #10 of the same NEIPA, you'll notice things your brew #1 self never saw:

- The dough-ball that always forms in the same corner of the mash tun
- The 5-minute window where the boil over wants to happen
- The exact dough/dough phase change at 60% attenuation
- What "perfectly chilled at 18.0 °C" really feels like for transfer

This pattern recognition is what makes professional brewers consistent. **Repetition unlocks it.**

### Iteration with control

When you change one variable on a recipe you know well, **you can attribute the change in the beer to that variable**. When you brew a totally new recipe, you don't know if a difference came from the recipe, the technique, the day, or chance.

For example: brewing the same NEIPA recipe but **swapping out one hop variety** in the dry hop. You'll learn what that variety actually contributes — far more than reading variety descriptions.

### Fewer ingredient runs out

A rotation lets you **buy in batches** — 1 lb bags of your core hops, multi-batch grain orders, a half-case of yeast at once. Cost savings + always-on-hand reliability.

### Friends and family expectations

People who drink your beer will start to remember your beers. **A signature pizza-night IPA** is more meaningful than 20 different one-off beers.

## Building a rotation

A reasonable rotation is **3–5 recipes** that:

- Cover different occasions / moods
- Span techniques (so you stay sharp on the full process)
- Use overlapping ingredients (efficient stocking)
- Match your fermenter availability

### Suggested 4-recipe rotation for a NEIPA-focused brewer

| Recipe                          | Frequency     | Role                                 |
|---------------------------------|---------------|--------------------------------------|
| **House NEIPA** (your standard)  | Every 3 weeks | Daily-driver IPA                     |
| **Hazy DIPA** (the stronger version) | Once / 2 months | Special-occasion big beer        |
| **Modern WCIPA** (clear, dry, hop-forward) | Once / 6 weeks | Variety, food beer            |
| **Cold IPA or Pilsner** (cold-fermented) | Once / 2 months | Lager skill, summer beer        |

This rotation produces **roughly 18–20 batches per year**. That's enough for a lot of pour and a lot of practice.

### Alternative rotation: minimal-style

For a brewer who wants depth-over-breadth and brews less often:

| Recipe                          | Frequency     | Role                                 |
|---------------------------------|---------------|--------------------------------------|
| **House NEIPA**                 | Every 4 weeks | The only IPA you brew                |
| **Soft hazy session IPA** (~5%) | Every 6 weeks | Lighter alternative                  |

8–10 batches/year. Simpler.

### Alternative rotation: variety-leaning

For a brewer who values style breadth:

| Recipe                          | Frequency     | Role                                 |
|---------------------------------|---------------|--------------------------------------|
| **House NEIPA**                 | Every 4 weeks | IPA cornerstone                      |
| **Cold IPA**                    | Every 6 weeks | Lager skill                          |
| **Saison or Brett saison**      | Every 8 weeks | Yeast-driven variety                 |
| **Stout or porter**             | Every 8 weeks | Dark-beer rotation                   |
| **Hazy DIPA**                   | Special occasions | Big beer                         |

12–15 batches/year. More variety, less depth per recipe.

## The "House NEIPA" — your foundation

If you brew one recipe more than any other, **make it your House NEIPA**. This is where you should iterate, refine, and develop signature character.

A house NEIPA recipe typically:

- **Uses 3–4 hops you trust** — your "house combo"
- **Sticks to one yeast strain** — Verdant IPA recommended
- **Locked grain bill** — pilsner + oats + wheat at fixed proportions
- **Locked water** — your tap-adjusted recipe
- **Variable across batches:** hop ratios, dry hop timing, total hopping rate

Example House NEIPA framework:

```
- Pilsner: 65%
- Oats: 15%
- Wheat: 15%
- Carapils: 5%

- Whirlpool: Citra 50% + Mosaic 30% + Galaxy 20% (~6 g/L)
- Active-ferm DH: Citra 50% + Galaxy 50% (~4 g/L)
- Cold-side DH: rotating - try different hop combos here

- Yeast: Verdant IPA, 11g
- Water: tap-adjusted to NEIPA spec
- 18 °C ferment, 13 °C cold-side DH (3 days)
```

The cold-side dry hop is where you **iterate**:

- Batch 1: Citra + Mosaic + Amarillo
- Batch 2: Citra + Mosaic + Strata
- Batch 3: Citra + Galaxy + Riwaka
- Batch 4: Mosaic + El Dorado + Idaho 7
- ...

After 6–10 iterations, you'll know exactly what cold-side combo you love.

## How to iterate

The deliberate-iteration approach:

### 1. Establish a baseline

Brew the recipe **3 times in a row exactly the same way**. Drink each, take notes, identify what's consistent vs what's noise.

### 2. Change one variable per iteration

Then for each subsequent batch, change **one thing**:

- **Single hop substitution** in cold-side DH
- **Total hop rate** (try +25% or -25%)
- **Water adjustment** (more sulfate, less chloride)
- **Yeast change** (try Conan instead of Verdant; same recipe otherwise)
- **Mash temp** (try 65 vs 67 vs 68 °C)
- **Bulk fermentation temp** (18 vs 21 °C)
- **Cold-side DH temp** (13 vs 18 °C)

**Don't change two things at once.** You won't know which caused what.

### 3. Notes matter

After each iteration, write down:

- What you changed
- What you observed during the brew
- How the beer turned out (specific descriptors, not "great")
- Whether you'd repeat the change

### 4. Update the recipe when an iteration is "better"

When you find something you prefer, **update your House NEIPA recipe** to incorporate it. Then iterate from the new baseline.

Over 12 months, your House NEIPA will have evolved through 6–10 iterations. **You'll have a recipe better than anything you'd have downloaded online**, because it's tuned to your taste, your equipment, your water.

## Building a "house" identity

Over 12–18 months of iteration, you'll develop:

- **Signature hop combinations** — what you reach for
- **Preferred mash temp / yeast / water** — your defaults
- **Distinctive style elements** — slightly drier finish, slightly higher oats, etc.
- **Friends who request "your beer"** — by name

This is **your style as a brewer**. Don't undervalue it.

## Common rotation mistakes

- **Brewing 8 different recipes in 3 months.** No iteration; no mastery.
- **Tweaking everything at once.** Can't attribute results.
- **Skipping the notes.** Memory is unreliable.
- **Chasing other people's beers** instead of making your own. The "I want to make Tree House Julius" is a guide; making your own version that you love more is the goal.
- **Stopping iteration once a recipe is "good enough."** Even great recipes can get better. Once you stop iterating, you stop learning.
- **Trying to maintain too many house recipes.** 5+ recipes = each one only brewed twice a year. Insufficient repetition for mastery.

## When to break out of the rotation

**Sometimes** brew a one-off:

- A new style you've never tried (Saison, Stout, Brett aging)
- A hop you've heard about and want to evaluate
- A friend's recipe you want to taste
- A seasonal / holiday-specific beer

**This is healthy.** Rotation isn't a prison; it's a skill-building tool.

But the **majority of your brewing** should still be your house rotation. The one-offs are for breadth; the rotation is for depth.

## Recipes as scaffolding

Good house recipes are **frameworks**, not fixed instructions. They have:

- **Locked elements** — grain bill, water, yeast (don't change without good reason)
- **Variable elements** — specific hop choices, dry hop ratios (test these)
- **Tracked metrics** — OG, FG, mash pH, fermentation timing

Each iteration **shifts a variable element** and produces data on what works.

This approach combines **discipline** (the recipe is mostly the same) with **exploration** (you're always learning something).
