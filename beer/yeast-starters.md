# Yeast starters

A "starter" is a small **pre-fermentation step** that **multiplies yeast cells before pitching into your main wort**. It exists because **a single liquid-yeast pack rarely contains enough cells** to ferment a typical homebrew batch cleanly, especially anything above 1.050 OG.

If you're using **dry yeast** (Lallemand Verdant IPA, Safale US-05, etc.), **you almost never need a starter** — that's most of the convenience advantage right there. This file explains starters for completeness, and explains the rare cases where even dry yeast brewers benefit from one.

## TL;DR by yeast type

| Yeast type                 | Need a starter?                                          |
|----------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| **Dry yeast (any)**        | **No** for OG ≤ 1.075. Just pitch the sachet.            |
| **Dry yeast (high OG)**    | **No** — pitch a second sachet instead of making a starter |
| **Liquid yeast (1 vial)**  | **Yes for OG ≥ 1.055** — almost always recommended       |
| **Liquid yeast (low OG)**  | Optional for OG ≤ 1.050; many brewers skip               |
| **Repitched yeast slurry** | Sometimes — if slurry is small or old                    |
| **Saved/banked yeast**     | **Yes** to verify viability and bulk up cell count       |

## Why starters exist (the cell count problem)

Yeast fermentation works best with the right cell count for your wort:

- **Too few cells** = stressed yeast, slow lag, off-flavors, possible stuck fermentation
- **Too many cells** = autolysis (yeast cells dying and producing off-flavors)
- **Goldilocks zone** = clean, vigorous fermentation, good attenuation, no off-flavors

The rough target for **ales** is **0.75 million cells per mL per °P** of wort. For a 23 L (5 gal) batch at 1.060 OG (~14.7 °P):

$$
0.75 \times 10^6 \text{ cells/mL/°P} \times 23{,}000 \text{ mL} \times 14.7 \text{ °P} \approx 254 \text{ billion cells}
$$

A typical liquid yeast vial contains **100 billion cells at packaging**, declining to maybe **70–80 billion** by the time you brew (cells die in storage at ~1% per day after manufacture).

So one fresh vial = roughly **30–40% of what you actually need.** Hence the starter to multiply cells.

A typical dry yeast packet (11g) contains **~220 billion cells** when fresh — already at the right pitch rate for most ales without a starter.

## Equipment for making starters

- **Erlenmeyer flask** (1.5 L or 2 L) — sized to your typical starter volume
- **Foam stopper or aluminum foil** — covers the flask, allows CO₂ out, keeps contaminants out
- **Stir plate** (recommended) — magnetic stirrer with adjustable speed; circulates wort and aerates
- **Magnetic stir bar** — sits in the flask, spun by the stir plate
- **Dry malt extract (DME)** — the easiest starter wort base
- **Optional: yeast nutrient** (Wyeast Nutrient or similar)
- **Sanitizer** (Star San) — everything that touches the starter must be sanitized

A basic stir plate setup costs **$60–100**. Without one, you can shake the flask manually every few hours, which works but is less effective.

## How to make a starter

### Step 1: Calculate the size

Online calculators (Brewer's Friend, Brewfather) compute exact starter size based on:

- Pack age (cell viability declines with time)
- Original cell count (vials = 100B, smack packs = 100B, Imperial = 200B)
- Wort gravity and volume target
- Whether you have a stir plate (more efficient)

Typical starter sizes:

- **For 23 L NEIPA at 1.065 with a fresh vial:** ~1.5–2 L starter
- **For 23 L DIPA at 1.080 with a fresh vial:** 2.5–3 L starter (or 2 vials with smaller starter)
- **For 23 L lager / Pilsner at 1.050:** ~2 L starter
- **For 23 L Cold IPA at 1.065:** 2.5–3 L starter (lager yeast needs more cells)

### Step 2: Make starter wort

Standard ratio: **100 g DME per 1 L of water = 1.040 OG starter wort**.

Procedure:

1. **Boil** the DME + water for **10 minutes** in a clean pot. (Sanitizes; dissolves DME; drives off any oxidized character.)
2. **Optional:** add a pinch of yeast nutrient.
3. **Cool to ~22 °C** (chill bath in sink with cold water + ice).
4. **Pour into sanitized flask.**

### Step 3: Pitch and stir

1. **Sanitize the yeast pack exterior** (spray with Star San).
2. **Open and pour the yeast** into the cooled wort.
3. **Add the sanitized magnetic stir bar.**
4. **Cover with foam stopper or foil.**
5. **Place on stir plate** at moderate speed (vortex visible but not violent).
6. **Let it stir for 18–36 hours** at room temp (22–24 °C).

### Step 4: Use the starter

Two approaches:

#### Approach A: Pitch the whole thing

- Wait until starter has fermented out (no more activity, foam settled)
- Pour the entire starter — wort + yeast — into your main wort
- **Diluting effect:** adds 1–2 L of fermented starter wort to your batch
- Slight off-flavor risk from spent starter

#### Approach B: Decant (preferred)

- After fermentation, **refrigerate the starter for 24+ hours**
- Yeast settles to the bottom
- **Carefully decant the spent starter wort** off the top, leaving only the yeast slurry
- Pitch just the slurry into your main wort
- Cleaner, no dilution

Approach B is the standard practice for serious homebrewers.

### Starter day-by-day

For a Saturday brew day:

- **Wednesday evening:** make starter, pitch yeast, on stir plate
- **Friday evening:** starter activity has finished; refrigerate
- **Saturday morning:** decant spent wort, pitch slurry on brew day

## When to skip starters (and use multiple sachets / vials instead)

A starter takes **3–4 days of planning**. Sometimes faster alternatives win:

### For dry yeast brewers (most cases)

- **Standard NEIPA at 1.065:** one 11g sachet (Verdant IPA) — done
- **DIPA at 1.080:** **two sachets** (22g) — done
- **Imperial at 1.090+:** **three sachets** (33g) — done

Cost: ~$5 per sachet vs ~$2–3 of DME for a starter. Three sachets = $15. A starter = $3. But the starter requires equipment, time, and a stir plate.

For most home NEIPA brewers: **multiple sachets is faster and easier** than starters.

### For liquid yeast brewers

- **Two vials with no starter** approximates a 1 vial + 1.5 L starter for a moderate ale
- Saves the 3-day starter wait
- Costs more (two vials = $20+)
- Many brewers do this for time savings

## When even dry yeast brewers benefit from a starter

Rare but real cases:

- **Very high OG** (1.090+) — even 22g of dry yeast may benefit from being "woken up" in starter wort first to ensure full viability
- **Old dry yeast** (past expiration) — starter confirms viability before pitching into a full batch
- **Banked / saved yeast** from a previous brew — bulk up cell count before pitching
- **Lager fermentation** — dry lager yeast at high OG sometimes benefits from a stepping-up starter
- **Mixed-fermentation primary** — starting Brett or LAB cultures in a small starter ensures activity

For a typical 6.5–7% NEIPA with fresh Verdant IPA, **none of these apply**. Just pitch and brew.

## "Vitality starter" — a hybrid for dry yeast

A simplified middle ground: rehydrate dry yeast in **a tiny amount of starter wort** for an hour before pitching:

1. Boil 100 mL of water + 10g DME, cool to 25 °C
2. Sanitize a small flask or jar
3. Pour wort in, **sprinkle dry yeast** on top
4. Wait 30–60 minutes — yeast should bloom (fluff up, slight bubbling)
5. Pitch into main wort

This isn't a true starter (no cell multiplication), but it **wakes the dry yeast up** before stress-testing it on full wort. Some brewers do this routinely for high-OG batches; others find it unnecessary.

## Common starter mistakes

- **Stir plate too violent.** Vortex should be gentle; aggressive whirlpooling damages cells.
- **Starter too thin (low OG).** Below 1.030 starters don't multiply cells effectively. Stay at 1.040.
- **Starter too thick.** Above 1.050 stresses yeast. Stay at 1.040.
- **Insufficient nutrient.** DME alone is OK; adding yeast nutrient helps cell health.
- **Pitching warm wort onto cold starter slurry.** Temperature shock kills cells. Match temps before pitching.
- **Starter contaminated.** Foul smell (vomit, sulfur unrelated to ferment) = throw out, start over.
- **Skipping refrigeration before decant.** Yeast doesn't settle; you decant viable yeast off the top.
- **Old starter unused.** A starter sitting unused for 5+ days is past peak; pitch within 3 days.

## When a starter is genuinely worth the effort

You should make a starter when:

- **You're using fresh liquid yeast for a moderate-to-high OG ale or any lager** — this is the canonical use case
- **You want a specific liquid strain** that isn't available dry
- **You're confident in your sanitation** — the starter step is one more contamination opportunity
- **You have the timing flexibility** to plan 3–4 days ahead

Don't make a starter when:

- **You're using fresh dry yeast at moderate OG** — just pitch the sachet
- **You're time-constrained** for a brew day
- **You're new to brewing** — adding complexity early is a recipe for sanitation failures

## Recommendation for a dry-yeast NEIPA brewer

If you're committed to dry yeast (which is most NEIPA brewers' practical reality):

- **Standard NEIPA / WCIPA / Pale Ale (OG ≤ 1.075):** one Verdant IPA sachet. No starter.
- **DIPA / Imperial IPA (OG 1.080–1.095):** two Verdant IPA sachets. No starter.
- **Cold IPA / Pilsner (lager yeast):** two W-34/70 sachets. Optional rehydration in 100 mL warm water for 15 min.
- **Imperial Stout / Barleywine (OG 1.090+):** two or three sachets. Consider a vitality rehydration step.

This entire workflow **bypasses starter equipment and the 3-day timeline.** It's the convenience case for dry yeast.

If you ever want to use liquid yeast for a specific strain (chasing a brewery clone, doing a Brett-aged saison, etc.) — **then make a starter**. But for routine NEIPA brewing, you can run an entire brewing operation without ever making a starter.
