## Mental models for hop usage

Three things to understand before designing a hop schedule:

### 1. You're spending an oil budget

Every hop addition has a **flavor and aroma payoff** and a **cost** (bitterness, polyphenol load, hop creep risk, raw material cost). Whirlpool and dry hopping are *not interchangeable* — they extract different things and lose different things.

- **Bittering boil (60+ min)** — alpha acids isomerize to iso-α-acids = bitterness. Volatile oils boil off. Cheap to add IBUs here.
- **Late kettle / flameout** — some isomerization, some oil retention. Useful but inefficient.
- **Whirlpool / hopstand at 170 °F (77 °C)** — minimal isomerization, maximum oil retention. The sweet spot for big aroma without big bitterness.
- **Active-fermentation dry hop** — biotransformation. Yeast enzymes convert hop precursors into new aromatic compounds. Hop creep risk is real.
- **Cold-side dry hop** — pure aroma extraction. No biotransformation. Brightest, freshest impression. Most exposed to oxidation.

### 2. Hops have an oil profile, not a flavor

When variety sheets list "tropical," "citrus," "dank" — they're shorthand for an underlying **oil composition** (myrcene, humulene, caryophyllene, farnesene, linalool, geraniol, terpene thiols). Two hops with similar descriptor profiles can behave very differently because their underlying oils respond differently to heat, time, yeast, and oxygen.

For example:
- **Citra and Mosaic** both list as "tropical" — but Citra is geraniol-rich (yeast can biotransform) while Mosaic relies more on heat-sensitive thiols (whirlpool > biotransformation).
- This is why **the same hop can taste different in two recipes** — the *technique* changed what you extracted.

### 3. The hop / yeast / water triangle

You can't think about hops in isolation. Three legs:

- **The hop variety** (oil profile)
- **The yeast** (which oils it can biotransform, what esters it adds)
- **The water** (chloride softens hops, sulfate sharpens; pH affects polyphenol extraction)

A NEIPA recipe is a coordinated choice across all three. A "great hop combo" pairing fails on the wrong yeast, or with the wrong water.

## Quick recipe-design heuristics

For NEIPA specifically:

- **Whirlpool addition: 4–8 g/L** total, at 75–80 °C, contact 20–30 min.
- **Active-ferm dry hop: 4–8 g/L**, dropped at 50–70% attenuation (typically 24–48 hr in).
- **Cold-side dry hop: 4–8 g/L**, dropped at FG, contact 2–4 days at 18 °C, then crash.
- **Total hopping rate: 12–20 g/L** is typical. 25 g/L pushes "haze bomb" territory and risks polyphenol astringency.
- **Use 3–5 varieties** in a coordinated combo. Single-hop NEIPAs are fine but harder to make distinctive.

These are starting points — see [`dry-hopping.md`](dry-hopping.md) for the why and the variations.
