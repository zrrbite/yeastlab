# Hop sensory analysis

How to evaluate hops with your nose and mouth — before you brew with them, after you brew with them, and as you build a personal hop reference. The single fastest way to get better at IPA brewing is to **train your sensory system** alongside your brewing system.

This file is a framework, not a one-time guide. Use it for every new hop variety you encounter, every hop comparison you do, and every beer you brew.

## Why bother

Three reasons every IPA brewer should do structured sensory work:

1. **Spec sheets lie.** Yakima Chief's spec sheet says Citra is "stone fruit, citrus, tropical." That's true *on average* across the variety. Your specific bag of Citra from a specific 2024 harvest could be more melon, more onion-leaning, or more grapefruit-pith. Your nose tells you what the bag actually has; the spec sheet tells you what the variety usually is.

2. **Your descriptors are yours.** "Mango" means different things to different people. Building a personal hop vocabulary — anchored to specific hops you've smelled — produces better recipe design than memorizing other people's words.

3. **Sensory degradation is the silent killer.** A hop that smells slightly off in the bag will smell *more* off in the beer. If you can't tell a fresh hop from an aged one by smell, you'll waste money and brew bad beer until you can.

## The four sensory contexts

Hops can be evaluated in four progressively-more-realistic settings:

| Setting              | What it tells you                                | When to use                         |
|---------------------|--------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Dry rub**          | Headline aromatics, age/oxidation status         | Buying / receiving hops             |
| **Tea (cold water)** | What's water-soluble at low temp; cleaner profile | First-look variety evaluation       |
| **Hop tea (hot water)** | What's extracted at brewing temps             | Pre-brew check before whirlpool     |
| **In beer**          | Reality. Multi-compound interactions, full context | Post-brew, comparing brews          |

You don't have to do all four for every hop. But for varieties you're new to or care about, **do all four at least once**. The mapping between "smells like X dry" → "tastes like Y in beer" is real and you can learn it.

## 1. Dry rub

The fastest test. Used to:

- Assess **freshness** (the most important quality check)
- Get a quick read on the **headline aromatics**
- Detect **off-aromas** (onion, garlic, cardboard, cheese)

### Procedure

1. **Open the bag.** Inhale the **headspace aroma** first — what's volatized into the bag.
2. **Take a small pinch** of pellets (3–5 g).
3. **Rub them between your palms** for 5–10 seconds with moderate pressure. The friction releases volatile oils from the lupulin.
4. **Cup your hands around your nose** and inhale deeply.
5. **Wait 30 seconds, smell again.** Different compounds volatilize at different rates — you'll get a different aromatic profile after a brief wait.

### What to record

- **Initial headspace aroma:** what you smelled when first opening the bag
- **First-rub aroma:** the immediate punch
- **30-second-later aroma:** what's left after the volatile top notes fade
- **Overall intensity:** 1–10 scale
- **Specific descriptors:** as many as you can pull out
- **Any off-aromas:** onion, garlic, cardboard, cheese, mushroom

### What to look for

**Fresh, healthy hop:** vivid, characteristic, clean, layered. Distinct top notes (often green/citrus) that fade to deeper layers (resin, dank, earth).

**Slightly aged hop:** still recognizable but muted. Less "punch." Top notes faded.

**Oxidized hop:** flat, generic-hop smell with hints of cardboard or paper. Specific varietal character barely present.

**Onion/garlic-aged Citra/Simcoe:** unmistakable sulfur character — like aged garlic powder or oxidized onion. **Don't dry hop with this.** Bittering use only.

**Cheese-aged:** isovaleric acid character. Bad. Compost.

## 2. Cold water tea

A more "objective" test than dry rub. Removes the variable of friction-extracted oils and shows what dissolves into water at room temp.

### Procedure

1. **Take 5 g of pellets.** Crumble into 200 mL of room-temperature filtered water in a glass.
2. **Stir gently.** Pellets will dissolve / disperse.
3. **Wait 5 minutes.**
4. **Smell the headspace.** Don't taste yet.

### What to record

- Aroma at 5 min: descriptors
- Aroma at 15 min: how it has shifted
- **Optional taste test** (if you trust your hops are clean): a small sip. Note bitterness, mouthfeel, aftertaste.

### What this reveals

- Hop oils that are **water-soluble at low temp** — primarily monoterpene alcohols (linalool, geraniol) and the polar fraction of hop oils
- **Cleaner signal** than dry rub — fewer compound classes activated
- **Subtle differences between similar hops** — Citra vs Simcoe at this test reveals their underlying differences more clearly than dry rub does

### Why this matters for dry hopping

Janish's research shows that **cold dry hopping (4–13 °C) extracts mostly monoterpene alcohols** while warmer dry hopping (>20 °C) extracts more hydrocarbons (myrcene). The cold water tea is essentially a model of the cold dry hop — it gives you a preview of what will end up in your cool dry hop.

If a hop tastes great in cold water tea: it'll dry hop well at low temp.
If a hop is "muted" in cold water but explodes when you smell the dry rub: it has a lot of myrcene/hydrocarbons, which means warm dry hop will pull more of its character (along with more polyphenols).

This is an actually useful diagnostic.

## 3. Hot hop tea

Models the whirlpool and hot-side hop additions.

### Procedure

1. **Take 5 g of pellets.**
2. **Pour 250 mL of just-off-boil water (~80 °C / 175 °F)** into a glass mug.
3. **Add the pellets, stir.**
4. **Cover with a saucer.**
5. **Wait 15 minutes.**
6. **Uncover, let cool 1–2 minutes** (don't burn your nose).
7. **Smell the headspace.**

### What to record

- Aroma intensity (should be much stronger than cold tea)
- Specific descriptors
- Taste test: bitterness level (will be moderate — some isomerization at 80 °C), aroma in the mouth, aftertaste

### What this reveals

- The **survivable hot-side compounds** — esters (isoamyl isobutyrate, methyl geranate, 2MIB), monoterpene alcohols, and the more thermal-stable thiol precursors
- A **preview of what whirlpool will deliver** to the fermenter
- Compounds that boil off (myrcene at high concentrations) will be less prominent here than in dry rub

### Why this matters for whirlpool

Janish (2021) recommends whirlpool at **180 °F / 82 °C** specifically because it preserves the survivable compounds while still allowing some isomerization. If a hop tea at 80 °C smells great, it'll work great in whirlpool. If it tastes flat or harsh, that hop is better used elsewhere (bittering only, or dry hop only).

## 4. In beer

The test that matters most — but the slowest. Best done as **single-hop side-by-sides**.

See [`../recipes/single-hop-experiments.md`](../recipes/single-hop-experiments.md) for the full single-hop framework.

The key insight: **only by tasting a hop alone in a finished beer can you know what it actually contributes** — separated from the other hops in your usual recipes.

### Tasting framework for a finished beer

1. **Pour into identical glasses** at the same temperature (8–10 °C is good for IPAs).
2. **Look:** color, clarity, head retention, lacing.
3. **Smell first, before tasting:**
   - Initial sniff: what hits first
   - Deeper sniff: what's underneath
   - Swirl, smell again: lifted volatiles
4. **Taste:**
   - First sip: don't analyze, just react
   - Second sip: focus on aroma in the mouth
   - Third sip: focus on bitterness — character, intensity, where it appears (front of tongue? back?)
   - Fourth sip: focus on finish — what lingers? for how long?
5. **Re-taste at two temperatures:**
   - Cold (8 °C): tight, integrated
   - Warmer (12 °C): more aromatic, more revealing

### Structured tasting form (per beer)

```
Beer name / hop bill: __________
Tasting date: __________
Pour temp: __________

Visual:
  Color: _____ SRM (estimated)
  Clarity: brilliant / clear / slight haze / hazy / opaque
  Head: thin / moderate / thick; white / off-white / cream
  Lacing: minimal / moderate / heavy

Aroma intensity (1–5): _____
Aroma descriptors (free-form):
  Pre-tasting smell:
    ________________________________________
  After swirl:
    ________________________________________

Flavor descriptors:
  ________________________________________

Mouthfeel:
  Body: thin / light / medium / full / heavy
  Carbonation: low / moderate / high / spritzy
  Texture: silky / smooth / oily / dry / drying

Bitterness:
  Intensity (1–5): _____
  Character: clean / pine / grapefruit / herbal / harsh / vegetal
  Where felt: front / mid / back of palate
  Finish duration: short / medium / lingering

Overall impression:
  ________________________________________

What I'd change about this beer:
  ________________________________________
```

A few of these per beer over a tasting session, and over time you build a personal database that's worth more than any external review.

## Building a personal hop vocabulary

The descriptors in spec sheets ("tropical, citrus, dank") are **other people's words for what they smelled**. Yours might be different — and that's fine, as long as you're consistent.

### How to build your own

1. **Pick 5–10 reference smells you know well**. For me: orange peel (zest), peach (ripe), cannabis flower, fresh-cut pine needles, white grape (Sauvignon Blanc), gooseberry, white peach, lychee, grapefruit pith.
2. **Anchor your hop descriptors to these references**. "Citra reminds me of lychee + orange peel + slight cat-pee." That's more useful than "tropical."
3. **Make hop-specific notes consistently**. Use the same vocabulary across hops over time.
4. **Re-calibrate yearly**. Your nose changes; your reference set will too.

### Common descriptor families and what they typically mean

| Descriptor family       | Typical chemistry                                    | Common hops                          |
|-------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Tropical fruit**      | Thiols (3MH, 4MMP), some hop esters                  | Citra, Galaxy, Mosaic, Strata        |
| **Citrus (zest)**       | Linalool, limonene, geraniol                         | Cascade, Centennial, Amarillo        |
| **Citrus (rind/pith)**  | Combined citrus monoterpenes, some humulene          | Centennial, Cascade, Chinook         |
| **Stone fruit (peach)** | Some specific esters, secondary thiols                | Amarillo, Vermont Ale yeast (combination) |
| **Pine / resin**        | Caryophyllene, humulene, alpha-pinene                | Simcoe, Chinook, Cascade             |
| **Dank / cannabis**     | Specific terpenes (myrcene + caryophyllene combo)    | Mosaic, Strata, Simcoe               |
| **Floral**              | Linalool, geraniol, low-myrcene varieties            | Loral, Sterling, Willamette          |
| **Coconut / cream**     | Specific high-geraniol, some methyl esters           | Sabro                                |
| **Wine (white grape)**  | 3MH thiol (Sauvignon-Blanc-like)                     | Nelson Sauvin, Hallertau Blanc       |
| **Berry**               | Some thiols, ester combinations                       | Mosaic, Strata                       |
| **Herbal / earthy**     | Humulene, farnesene                                  | Loral, Saaz, Tettnanger              |
| **Onion / garlic**      | Sulfur compound oxidation (degraded thiols)          | Aged Citra, Simcoe                   |

The chemistry → descriptor mapping is real but imperfect. Use it as orientation, not gospel.

## Hop comparisons — methodology

When evaluating two hops side by side:

### Same-format comparison

- Both T-90 (or both Cryo, etc.)
- Same year of harvest
- Same supplier (or at least same reputable source)
- Same evaluation method (both dry rub, both tea, etc.)

### What to compare

- **Aroma intensity** — which is louder?
- **Specific descriptors** — what's distinct about each?
- **Where they overlap** — what do they share?
- **What's missing in one vs the other**

### A useful exercise: "if I had to pick three descriptors for each"

Force yourself to choose. Citra → "lychee, orange peel, mild grapefruit." Mosaic → "blueberry, dank, pineapple-fading." This forces specificity and is more useful than a 10-descriptor blob.

## Tracking degradation over time

Over a 12-month period:

- **Buy a 1-lb bag** of a major hop (Citra, Mosaic, Galaxy)
- **Vacuum-seal subdivisions** of 50 g each, immediately on receipt
- **Open one** every 2 months
- **Do all four sensory tests** (dry rub, cold tea, hot tea, brewing in a single-hop beer)
- **Track in a notebook**

After a year you'll have a definitive record of how that variety degrades over time — and your nose will be calibrated to detect the early signs of degradation in any hop.

## Common sensory mistakes

- **Tasting too cold.** IPAs at < 6 °C suppress aromatics significantly. 8–10 °C is the sweet spot for evaluation.
- **Smell fatigue.** After 3–4 hops in a row, your nose is dead. Reset by smelling **your own forearm skin** (a neutral reference). Take a 10-minute break between samples for serious work.
- **Bias from labels.** Cover labels when tasting. You'll be surprised how often "Galaxy" turns out to be "Mosaic" when you're tasting blind.
- **Single sample syndrome.** A 5 g pinch from one bag isn't representative. Smell across the bag — pellet density and color can vary within one bag.
- **Skipping the cold water tea.** It's the easiest way to learn what cold dry hopping will deliver, and most brewers skip it.
- **Not writing things down.** Memory is unreliable; notes are not.
