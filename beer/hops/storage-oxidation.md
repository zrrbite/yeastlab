# Hop storage & oxidation

Hops degrade. From the moment they're picked, the clock is ticking on alpha acids, oils, and aromatic compounds. **Fresh hops in good condition are the single biggest variable in NEIPA quality** — bigger than recipe formulation, bigger than yeast strain, bigger than water profile. A great recipe with old hops will still taste old. A simple recipe with fresh hops will sing.

## How hops degrade

Three main pathways:

### 1. Alpha acid loss (humulone oxidation)

Alpha acids slowly oxidize into **humulinones** and other compounds. Humulinones are still bitter (~66% of iso-alpha bitterness, smoother) but they don't isomerize the same way during boiling.

**Practical impact:** an "old" hop has lower effective bittering potency than its label says. A 12% AA Citra at harvest may be 10% AA effective by month 12 of warm storage.

### 2. Cohumulone and oil oxidation → off-aromas

The aromatic compounds in hop oils oxidize into off-flavor compounds:

- **Onion / garlic / sweat character** — most notorious in **Citra** and **Simcoe**, but happens to many hops. Sulfur-containing compounds form from thiol oxidation.
- **Cardboard / sherry / oxidized character** — generic oxidation pathway, applies to hops in beer too.
- **Cheese / isovaleric acid** — older oxidation pathway, especially in poorly-stored hops.
- **"Old book" / paper / damp** — late-stage degradation.

These are **detectable in the dry hop sniff** before they go into the beer. **Always smell hops before you brew.** A hop that smells off in the bag will taste off in the beer.

### 3. Oil volatilization

The most volatile compounds (myrcene first) slowly leave the hop matrix even in vacuum-sealed packaging — though much more slowly than at room temperature. This is why even cold-stored hops gradually lose intensity over years.

## What controls degradation rate

Three variables, in rough order of impact:

### Temperature

The single biggest lever. Degradation rate roughly **doubles for every 10 °C increase**.

| Storage temp        | Effective shelf life (vacuum-sealed) |
|---------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Room temp (~22 °C)  | 3–6 months                            |
| Refrigerator (4 °C) | 12–18 months                          |
| Freezer (-18 °C)    | 24–36+ months                         |
| Liquid N₂ (-196 °C) | Years (commercial use only)           |

**Always store hops in the freezer.** Refrigerator-only is acceptable for varieties you'll use within 6 months. Room temp is for hops you've already lost.

### Oxygen

Oxygen accelerates every degradation pathway. Hops are **vacuum-packaged with N₂ flush** to minimize oxygen exposure during storage.

- **Sealed mylar bag (vacuum + N₂):** oxygen exposure near zero.
- **Zip-lock bag in freezer:** oxygen ingress is slow but real. ~6 months to noticeable degradation.
- **Open jar / loose packaging:** unusable within 1–2 months even cold.
- **Re-sealed after partial use:** much faster degradation than a fresh sealed bag.

**The first time you open a hop bag, you should use most of it.** Re-sealing extends life maybe 2–4 weeks at freezer temps, but the bag has lost its protective atmosphere.

### Light

UV degrades hop compounds — same chemistry as "skunked beer" (mercaptans from light-struck iso-alpha-acids). Less critical for whole hops in opaque packaging, but real:

- Keep hops in **opaque or foil-lined packaging**.
- If transferring to a clear container, **store in the dark**.
- Window-side freezer storage is fine for sealed mylar but bad for clear containers.

## Buying fresh hops

### Check the harvest year

US hop harvests are **August–September** (Pacific Northwest) and **March** (Australia/NZ). At any given time:

- **"Current crop"** (most recent harvest) — best quality.
- **"Last year's crop"** — usable, especially if frozen since harvest.
- **"Previous crops"** — buyer beware. Maybe OK for bittering, risky for aroma/dry hopping.

Reputable suppliers list the **harvest year on the bag** (e.g. "Harvest 2024"). If the bag doesn't say, assume worst case.

### Check the package

- **Vacuum bag should feel firm** — like a brick, not soft. Soft = O₂ leaked in.
- **Mylar (foil) packaging only** — paper bags or zip-locks are not acceptable for sustained storage.
- **Date stamps** — packaging date as well as harvest date.
- **Storage history** — buy from suppliers who store cold. (Online suppliers usually do; dusty homebrew shop shelves often don't.)

### Sources to trust

For US homebrewers:

- **Yakima Valley Hops** — direct from Yakima, fresh, fast.
- **Atlantic Brew Supply / Northern Brewer / Morebeer** — major homebrew retailers; turnover is fast enough that hops are usually fresh.
- **Yakima Chief Hops Direct** — recently expanded to homebrew quantities for some varieties.
- **Country Malt Group** — for bigger orders.
- **Local homebrew shops** — quality varies wildly. Check harvest dates and storage.

For European:

- **Lupex / Yakima Chief Europe** — fast, well-stored.
- **Hopsfromnz.com** — direct from NZ for the rare varieties.

Avoid Amazon for hops. Storage history is unknown.

### Buy quantities you'll use

Don't buy 1 lb of every variety because the price drops at the 1-lb threshold. **Buy what you'll use in 6 months.** A frozen lb of Citra still loses character over 18 months; better to buy a fresh ½-lb every few months.

For NEIPA brewers using big hop loads, this is less of a problem — a single batch can use 100–300 g per variety, so 1 lb (453 g) is 1–4 batches. Plan accordingly.

## Storing what you have

### At purchase

- **Open the shipping box, transfer to freezer immediately.** Don't let bags sit at room temp for days.
- Keep bags in their original vacuum/N₂ packaging until you need them.

### After opening a bag

If you've used part of a bag and need to store the rest:

1. **Squeeze out as much air as possible** before re-sealing.
2. **Vacuum-seal** if you have a vacuum sealer (FoodSaver-style). Best option.
3. **Mason jar with a vacuum lid attachment** — second best.
4. **Squeezed-flat zip-lock with extra air pressed out** — workable for a few weeks.
5. **Back into the freezer immediately.**

Realistically, **plan to use opened hops within 4–6 weeks** for full quality. Beyond that, they're still useful but you'll start to notice character drift.

### Tracking inventory

A spreadsheet or a notebook is enough:

- Variety
- Harvest year
- Quantity remaining
- Date opened (if applicable)
- Storage location (freezer A, freezer B, etc.)

Sounds fussy until you find a 3-year-old bag of Galaxy in the back of the freezer that's now expensive compost.

## How to recognize degraded hops

### Visual

- **Color shift** — fresh hops are bright green-yellow. Older hops fade toward gray-brown. Pellet color is less reliable than aroma.
- **Lupulin powder color** — should be golden-yellow. Brown lupulin is heavily oxidized.
- **Clumping or moisture** — pellets should be loose and dry. Sticky / clumped pellets have absorbed moisture, which accelerates degradation.

### Aromatic

This is the test. **Open the bag, smell.**

- **Fresh:** vibrant, characteristic of the variety, "alive."
- **Slightly aged:** muted but recognizable. Probably still usable for dry hopping.
- **Onion / garlic notes:** thiol oxidation. Citra and Simcoe especially. **Don't use for dry hopping** — the off-character will come through. OK for bittering only.
- **Cardboard / sherry / paper:** generic oxidation. Multiple compounds gone. Bittering-only at best.
- **Cheese / sweat / sock:** isovaleric acid. Bad. Compost.
- **No detectable hop aroma:** dead. Compost.

### A quick test for dry-hop suitability

Take 5–10 g of the hop in question, drop into 100 mL of cold water in a glass, swirl. Wait 5 min. Smell the headspace.

- Fresh hop: distinct varietal character, pleasant.
- Marginal hop: muted, dusty, generic-hop smell with hints of off-character.
- Bad hop: actively unpleasant — onion, cardboard, cheese.

If it doesn't pass the cold-water test, **don't dry hop with it.** You'll just amplify the off-character in the beer.

## Hop oxidation in the beer

Even fresh hops can produce oxidation off-flavors **after** they're in the beer if you mishandle DO at packaging:

- **Cardboard / sherry** in finished NEIPA almost always traces back to packaging-side oxidation, not the hops themselves.
- **Onion-garlic** that develops over 4–6 weeks in package suggests both: marginal hops + oxygen exposure compounded the issue.
- **Color darkening** (going from pale orange to brownish) is an oxidation signature.

See [`../process/packaging.md`](../process/packaging.md) for the closed-transfer playbook to keep finished beer fresh.

## A practical hop pantry strategy

For a NEIPA brewer doing 1 batch every 2–4 weeks:

1. **Stock 4–6 "core" varieties** in 1-lb quantities, frozen, vacuum-sealed: Citra, Mosaic, Galaxy, Simcoe, plus 1–2 rotating favorites.
2. **Add 2–4 "trial" varieties** in 4-oz quantities for experiments.
3. **Buy fresh every 3–4 months** to keep the rotation going.
4. **Use opened bags within 6 weeks.**
5. **Retire bags older than 18 months from freezer** for bittering-only use, or compost.

A modest hop pantry is ~4–6 lb total at any given time. That's two batches' worth of dry hop on the high end, plus some flex. More than that and you're storing inventory you'll lose to time.
