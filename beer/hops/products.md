# Hop products

The same hop variety comes in multiple physical formats, each with different oil density, polyphenol load, packaging, and price. For NEIPAs specifically, choosing the right format is one of the highest-leverage decisions you make.

## The format spectrum

### Whole cone (leaf)

Dried, intact hop cones. **Rare in modern brewing** outside of fresh-hop / wet-hop seasonal beers.

- **Pros:** unprocessed, less oxidation in storage if vacuum-sealed cold.
- **Cons:** bulky, low oil density per unit weight, hard to use in pressure fermenters, soaks up lots of beer.
- **NEIPA fit:** rarely. Maybe a "wet-hop" novelty release in September.

### T-90 pellets

The standard hop product. Whole cones milled and pelletized. ~90% of input mass retained — slight reduction in vegetal material. **The default for almost all brewing.**

- **Pros:** widely available, affordable, predictable, vacuum-packaged with N₂ flush, all hop compounds present.
- **Cons:** carries the full polyphenol and vegetal load — at high dry-hop rates this contributes to astringency, pH rise, and foam loss.
- **NEIPA fit:** **the workhorse**. 60–80% of your hop bill will likely be T-90 even in a heavily-Cryo'd recipe.

### T-45 pellets

Lupulin-enriched pellets. Cones are processed to remove some bract material before pelletizing — about 45% of the input mass retained, with **roughly 2× the lupulin density** of T-90 per unit weight.

- **Pros:** less leafy material per unit aroma → less polyphenol load, less pH rise.
- **Cons:** more expensive, less common (not all varieties available), some processing-related oxidation possible if not handled cold.
- **NEIPA fit:** **good complement** to T-90. Use for bittering or whirlpool when you want the AA without the leaf load. Not as game-changing as Cryo for dry hopping.

### Cryo Hops® / Lupulin powder (Yakima Chief)

Cones are processed at cryogenic temperatures (~-40 °C) to mechanically separate the **lupulin glands** (where the oils and acids live) from the bract material. The result is a yellow-green powder of nearly pure lupulin.

- **Roughly 2× the alpha acids and 2× the oils per unit weight** compared to T-90 of the same variety.
- **Dramatically less leaf/vegetal material.** Polyphenol load is much lower.
- Branded as **Cryo Hops®** by Yakima Chief Hops; **Lupomax®** by John I. Haas; "lupulin powder" generically.

**Pros:**

- **Less polyphenol load** → less astringency, less pH rise, better foam retention at heavy dry-hop rates.
- **Higher aroma per gram** → smaller volume of dry hop material absorbing beer in your fermenter cone.
- **Less vegetal flavor** at very high hop loads.
- Janish (2021) suggests Cryo / lupulin powder products **likely cause less of a pH increase** during dry hopping because the leaf material driving the pH rise is largely removed.

**Cons:**

- **Expensive** — typically 2–3× the cost per unit weight of T-90.
- **Not all varieties available** as Cryo. Most major hops are now offered (Citra, Mosaic, Simcoe, Galaxy, Strata, Sabro, El Dorado, Ekuanot, Idaho 7, etc.) but specialty hops may not be.
- **Easy to overdose.** Use roughly **half the rate** of the T-90 equivalent for the same intensity.
- Lupulin powder can clump and float on the wort/beer surface — agitation matters more than with T-90.

**NEIPA fit:** **excellent**, especially at high hopping rates (>14 g/L total). Mix with T-90 — don't go 100% Cryo because you lose some of the supporting compounds in the bract material that contribute to the "fullness" of perceived hop character.

### Spectrum® / Hop oil products / Liquid extracts

Concentrated **hop oil extracts** in a carrier (typically a small amount of water or ethanol). Branded as **Spectrum®** by Yakima Chief; **Incognito®** by John I. Haas; various other formulations.

- Oil concentrations are 50–100× T-90 by weight.
- Designed for **post-fermentation aroma additions** without solid hop material in the beer.

**Pros:**

- **Zero polyphenol or pH impact** — no leaf material at all.
- **No yield loss** — no spent hops absorbing beer in the cone.
- **Easy to dose precisely** for late-stage adjustments or "hop boost" additions to a finished beer.
- **Best storage stability** — concentrated, stable, easy to refrigerate.

**Cons:**

- **Limited variety availability** — Spectrum is offered in a handful of varieties.
- **Different sensory profile** than the cone — extraction process selects for certain compounds. Some brewers feel hop oil products taste "flatter" or "less complex" than equivalent dry hopping.
- **Expensive on a per-aroma-unit basis.**
- **Not a 1:1 replacement for dry hopping.** Should be paired with a normal dry hop for full character.

**NEIPA fit:** **niche but useful**. Great as a small late-stage "punch" addition to a beer that needs a bit more nose without more polyphenol load. Or for "shelf-life" additions just before packaging. Not a primary hop strategy.

### Hop creep mitigation: pre-isomerized extracts

Pre-isomerized alpha-acid extracts (iso-alpha extracts, "tetra-hop" extracts) are **liquid bittering products** with already-isomerized alpha acids.

- Used by some brewers for **clean bittering charges** without adding any hop solids to the kettle.
- **Don't bring biotransformable compounds**, hop oils, or polyphenols.
- Less common in modern craft / homebrew, more common in industrial macro brewing.

**NEIPA fit:** rarely. Most NEIPA brewers want some hop character even from the bittering charge.

## When to use what — a NEIPA hop bill format strategy

For a typical 14 g/L NEIPA, a sensible format mix:

| Addition           | T-90  | T-45 | Cryo  | Spectrum | Notes                                        |
|--------------------|-------|------|-------|----------|----------------------------------------------|
| Bittering (60 min) | ✓     | ✓    | —     | —        | T-90 or T-45. Cryo wastes oils via boiling.  |
| Whirlpool (180 °F) | ✓     | ✓    | ✓     | —        | Cryo cuts polyphenol load; T-90 still works. |
| Active-ferm DH     | ✓     | —    | ✓     | —        | T-90 fine for biotransformation; Cryo for cleanliness. |
| Cold-side DH       | ✓     | —    | ✓ ✓   | maybe    | Where Cryo really pays off. Spectrum optional for late punch. |
| Post-FG "punch"    | —     | —    | —     | ✓        | Optional — small addition just before kegging. |

For a heavy DIPA at 18–20 g/L, **lean harder into Cryo** for the cold-side dry hop. The polyphenol math at that hop load gets ugly with all-T-90.

## Cost-effectiveness math

A practical way to think about Cryo vs T-90:

- A standard NEIPA cold-side dry hop is **~5 g/L** of T-90 = ~115 g for a 23 L batch.
- The Cryo equivalent is **~2.5 g/L** = ~58 g for the same batch.
- If T-90 Citra is ~$40/lb (~$0.09/g) and Cryo Citra is ~$80/lb (~$0.18/g):
  - 115 g T-90 = **$10.35**
  - 58 g Cryo = **$10.44**

**Per dollar of hops, you're roughly even.** But you get less polyphenol, less pH rise, less hop material taking up cone space, and arguably brighter aroma. For NEIPA, **Cryo at parity cost is the better choice** for cold-side dry hopping.

(The math is less compelling for whirlpool — both work, T-90 has the slight edge in extracting "supporting" aroma from bract material at hot-side temps.)

## Buying and storage notes

- **Always check the harvest year and crop year** on the bag. Hops degrade fast — see [`storage-oxidation.md`](storage-oxidation.md).
- **Buy in vacuum-sealed mylar pouches** (not zip-lock bags). Even nitrogen-flushed packages should feel firm, not soft (oxygen would mean they've leaked).
- **Buy fresh, in quantities you'll use within 6 months.** Cold storage delays but doesn't stop degradation.
- **Cryo is more concentrated → smaller bags → easier to store cold without taking over your freezer.**
